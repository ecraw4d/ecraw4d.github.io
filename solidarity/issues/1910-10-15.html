A great disaster befell the people of Los Angeles on Saturday morning, Oct. 1, when the Los Angeles Times building was shattered by a terrific explosion and afterwards burned to the ground, carrying to death or injury nearly 80 workingmen and women. The death of these workingmen and women was terrible enough, but added to that was the accusation of the Times that the labor unions were responsible for the accident. The Times did not call it an accident, but they charged union men with deliberately dynamiting the building and killing the innocent men who were working in it. That charge made the situation worse, untruthful as it was. The explosion occurred on the morning of October 1 at 1 o'clock, just after the early shift had left the plant, leaving the night force of workers to the number of vest 100 men and women. There was such force behind the explosion that the four of the composing room on which was tor of the Times. After this the M. & M. started out to increase the reward for the capture of suspects to $100,000. The above is what appeared in the capitalist papers. Now let us think a little and sise up the situation for ourselves, after taking some facts into consideration. In the first place, the Times building might not have been dynamited at all. Experts with the use of explosives say that dynamite would not act in the manner which the explosive did in this case. Secondly, the Times building might have been blown up by an explosion of gas. A large number of the employes who escaped say that for several days there was an odor of gas throughout the building and that on the night of the explosion the odor was decidedly stronger, but that they were unable to find the leak. These employes also stated that gas was used in the building and that a large gas main enters through the cellar. The gas main might have sprung a leak and filled the cellar and the first floor with gas and this was in some. manner ignited. The action of the explosive was identically the same as gas. Last month word was sent me at Mc- Kees Rocks from Huberto Nieri, national secretary of the Italian Socialist Federation (the organisation that adheres neither to the S. P. or S. L. P., but really lives up to its duty to the Italian workers in this country by propagating industrial union ideas), urging me to come to New York, as, "given the bitter and sad ending of the cloak makers' strike, workers are clamoring for new methods of battle against the exploiters." Sept. 28 I left McKees Rocks and resched here the next day. I was at once met by groups of Italian workers who have lost all hopes in the yellow unions, and in the delusion that they can develop into red unions. Gray haired workers shook the by the hand and fold the story of gang of dirty labor fakirs, who just now are interested in creating a lot of fuss proclaiming general strikes of anything, from human hair workers to button hole workers; keep the workers out starving on the streets; collect thousands of dollars and then when it mes to giving any aid to the workers, make same kind of a dirty deal with the bosses whereby "recognition of the union," which for a few months at least insures the do-nousings an easy living, is conceded. Then they order the men and women back to work, proclaiming to the world of labor a general victory," as in the case of the clskmakers' strike, of which I will write me later. During the five days I was here last week, I succeeded in organizing the Italian Propaganda League with to militants whom I am confident we shall hear from in the near future. Spoke at a big meelist of shoe workers, who cheered the principles and direct action methods of the I. W. W. For John Tobin's union and its second edition, the "United Shoe Workers of America," there were sneers and derisions. One hundred and fifty signed the application for a charter under the I. W. W. The meeting resolved that everyone who had enrolled must become an organiser and bring one new applicant to the next meeting. Wednesday, after having addressed various meetings upon invitations of workers, I returned to MeKses Rocks, as important business had to be attended to. Friday I received word that at all cost I should be here for Sunday, as two big meetings had been arged for me to address-one of shoe werkers in Brooklyn and the other of ladies' custom tailors. Arrived here two hours late, but when I got the meeting the big crowd of shoe workers was still waiting. I spoke in English and in Italian; 104 new members were enrolled and all promised to bring more members to the next meeting. The eternal "delegate," a business agent of John Tobin's union, was in the meeting. I was told of him and his gang being present, so I did not spare the yellow and dirty outfit that, in the language of Tobin himself, agrees to unionize a shoe shop without changing the personel of the employes and at the "prevailing rate of wages." But not one of the 'diligats said a word. In the afternoon I again spoke in the two languages to the Ladies' Cuistom Tailors' Union, an independent body composed mostly of Italians. A long discussion followed my address to thes. A motion was made as follows: "Having heard and considered well the tactics, principles and ideals of the I. W. W., we immediately ask for a charter from said body to become an integral part of same." There were a few representatives of the old unions who tried to stay the vote, ar guing that the "I. W. W. is all right, but is too small," etc., but they were soon forced to change tact. Finally they be came threatening, and said that if these men would join the I. W. W., the A. F. of L. would line up with the bosses and the "radicals" will not have any work. They were answered by various workers, some in English and some in Italian. Upon the vote being taken, the resolution was carried, 55 votes yes, 4 no. This body has about 300 members. So much so far. But they act as irritants requiring much extra expenditure of money and energy to put the quietus to them, especially since the professional politicians will ever try to play the middle and capitalist classes against each other, for the politicians' own benefit. What more logical then, than for the corporations to apply "gumshoe" methods to the reform movements? All the while that the representatives of the Industrial Workers of the World in the strike regions of Westmoreland county were merely advising the miners to remain firm and fight it out to the end, without referring to other parts of the district being at work, they were tolerated by the officialdem of the United Mine Workers of America in District 5. And rather than create a fuss and get the worst of it they chose to lie low, waiting and praying for an opportunity to lay us low. At last, having to their minds secured the necessary excuse, the letter of Local Union 296, 1. W. W., of McKees Rocks, reprinted in No. 41 of Solidarity, they are now making what they deem the "best Fearing that the call for a general strike would find a response in the great mass of coal miners now working, who, if it were not for a contract existing between the officials of District 5, U. If they had a say in the matter a general strike would come tomorrow. They cannot see any justification in the world for allowing the miners of one part of the State to be out on strike against one set of masters, while the others (some of them working for the same interests) are allowed to dig coal and fill the orders of the struck companies. Unable to answer the arguments presented in the letter of Local Union 296, the organizers and officers of the Pittsburg district have set about, not to show the error of our way and position, but as usual to spreading lies and calumnies against our organization. Most of these false statements are too absurd to waste the space of Solidarity in repeating. They will only act as boomer angs against those who utter them. Spokane Locals, I. W. W. manager insists upon paying on the "five feet" seam, which is one shiling and nine pence per ton. Hence the strike of the 70 men immediately affected. When these men left, the manager gave notice to the other 900 miners in the same mine and locked them out. Then 2,000 men in a neighboring mine struck in sympathy. Now a conference is to be held in a week's time to try and settle matters. This is a typical case and altogether 50,000 men in Wales are very discontent- The boilermakers' case is as follows: When the 17 trade unions agreed to act together to arrive at a settlement of wages for a period of five years the boilermakers and ship platers (one union) protested against the decision arrived at, as it adversely affected them. But being in the minority, they were overridden and the agreement signed. Now that they find it reduces their power standard, the boilermakers refuse to work under it and many of the engineers are much dissatisfied also, and it may lead to a great stoppage of work. But it is a healthy sign the men are more determined than formerly to fight for better conditions. Largest Puddle Mill. At the new puddle mill of the Byers Co, Girard, O., 42 puddling furnaces are in operation and 46 are being erected. When these are completed, the plant will. consist of 88 single furnace, with 25 verticle boilers attached. This will be the largest and best equipped puddle mill in the country. There will be two trains of muck rolls, each with separate squeezer. The total capacity will be more than 200 tons of muck bar per day on double turn. Present output is about 100 tons per day. The plant is equipped with elevated electric trams, that deliver coal, iron and ore to the furnaces and remove cinder and ashes, loading them on cars at the same handling. All cranes, shears, lathes and other machinery are driven by electric power, the current being supplied by gas engines using blast furnace gas. One Belgian 12-inch skelp mill is in operation, and plans are completed for a skelp mill to roll wire plates, as the company is now intending to make large iron pipe.
