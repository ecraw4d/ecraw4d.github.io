THE WORKERS' POWER A portion of the working class has come to realize that it has a power of vital importance at its command that can bring the employing class, the owners of all tools of production, trembling and shaking to their kne's whenever the workers see fit on occasions of necessity for doing so. This form of power is known throughout France and among industrial unionists of America as SABOTAGE. A St. Louis paper som time ago commented on sabotage as the only weapon by which the workers could successfully ing to a victorious close what seemed to be hopelessly lost strike. The French workers turned defeat into a glorious victory by using it as a last resort. Sabotage is not always used consciously but also unconsciously. Take, for instance, workers in the hotels and restaurants. Bell boys and waiters are all proficient users of this force. Many waiters on seeing a "dead one" sitting at a table will ignore and avoid waiting on him, unless he wakes us and comes through with the ever expected tip. Their motto of "no tip, no service," manifests in itself their mode or code of attack. As for the bell boys, their environment moulds them into shrewd and cunning products, and woe to bim who does not "come through" for services shown. On occasions a bottle of ink may be found in some individual's ice water or a knife run through freshly starched shirts. Yes, they are all expert users of sabotage, though using it unconsciously or in a purely individualistic sense. Fashionable hotels and summer resorts, where the idle rich riot in luxury, are forever the bait of these workers. PREAMBLE The working class and the employing class have nothing in common. There can no peace so long as hunger and want are found among millions ef working people and the few, who make up the employing class, have all the good things of life. Between these two classes a struggle must go on until the workers of the world organize a class, take possession of the earth and the machinery of production, and abolish the wage system. We find that the centering of the management of industries into fewer and fewer hands makes the trades unions unable to cope with the ever-growing power of the employing class. The trade unions foster a state of affairs which allows one set of workers to be pitted against another set of workers in the same industry, thereby helping defeat one another in wage wars. Moreover, trade unions aid the employing class to mislead the workers into the belief that the working class have interests in common with their employers. These conditions can be changed and the interest of the working class upheld only by an organisation formed in such a way that all its members in industry, or in all industries if necessary, cease work whenever a strike or lockout is on in any department thereof, thus making an injury to one an injury to all. Instead of the conservative motto, "A fair day's wages for a fair day's work," we must inscribe on our banner the revolutionary watchword, "Abolition of the wage system." It is the historic mission of the working class to do away with capitalism. The army of production must be organized, not only for the every-day struggle with capitalists, but also to carry on production when capitalism shall have been overthrown. By organizing industrially we are forming the structure of the new society within the shell of the old. Knowing, therefore, that such an organization is absolutely necessary for emancipation we unite under the following constitution. That's what a member of the Industrial Workers of the World said yesterday at their headquarters on Sixth street. near the county jail. Vigorous nods of the heads on the part of the dozen other men in the room affirmed his declaration. The speaker continued: "There are 15 of our members in jail or on the farm, now. Within a week there'll be a hundred; that is, if the police keep on arresting is as we want them to. And we have got men coming from every part of the country to help keep the jail full. They're coming from Spokane and Fresno and Sacramento and Los Angeles, and orators rose up at all times of day and night and addressed their wildly enthusiastic fellow prisoners. The jail and stockade became perpetual bedlams of noise and seuffles between the prisoners and the army of specially sworn in deputies. As a last resort the fire department was called on and steams of ice cold mountain water poured on the frenzied fanatics. But the speaking continued, though both orators and auditors were drenched. For each man arrested a half dozen sprang up to take his place. While the excitement in Fresno and all California was at its height a group of 150 I. W. W. men from Spokane began a march to Fresno. They walked through Washington and Oregon to the California line, a mountain range high above the surrounding country, region of perpetual snow. But the "army" was not daunted by the snow. Slowly, but surely, they marched on. After weeks of marebing the "army" reached Sacramento, California's capital, and close to Fresno, their object point. Coxey's Lieutenant à Leader. In Sacramento they were received by a large force of I. W. W. men under Carl Browne, who was first lieutenant to Gen. Coxey on his famous march to Washington. Recruits poured in by the score and the augmented army prepared to leave for Fresno. But when the Fresno officials heard of the intended departure of the "army" from Sacramento they capitulated. The right of street speaking was granted the I. W. W., the jail was emptied and the stockade closed. Many of the I. W. W. marchers went on to Lower California, where they joined Gen.
