Among other subjects investigated and dealt with by this bunch of steel trust stockholders was the 12-hour workday. We quote from the report: "We are not unmindful of the fact that the 12-hour day has, by its general acceptance and practice over a considerable period of years, become firmly entrenched, and that any sudden or arbitrary change would involve a revolution in mill operations. Nor are we at all sure that it would be possible for any one employer, or any number of employers, to inaugurate shorter hour system, unless a similar policy should be adopted by all employers engaged in the same industry. We do believe that following in the wake of other betterment of conditions in the steel in dustry, there will naturally come a shortening of the hours of labor and the eventual abolishment of the 12-hour day, which will tend toward increasing the efficiency and resourcefulness of the working population and for that reason bring benefit to both employer and employed." Thus speak the beneficiaries of the 12- hour workday in the steel mills. Naturally enough, they offer no consolation to their slaves in the way of less hours of toil, unless, accompanying the shorter workday, they (the stockholders) can be sure of the "incressed efficiency and resourcefulness of the working population." Other than this, anything in the way of "hoped-for improvements" will be left, as the report naively suggests, "to the intelligent and thoughtful consideration of the proper officers of the corporation." It suggests in effect that a shorter workday cannot be established in a single mill, or in any number of mills; but must be a universal move against the entire corporation. That is ex- How? Gary stands out today as the greatest single manifestation of industrial power to be found in America The Steel Corporation's triumphs in the economics of production are only less impressive than its complete control over the army of workers it employs. These together are exceeded in significance at Gary only by the unparalleled opportunity this vast industrial power possesses to determine the living conditions of a great multitude of human beings. At Gary, as nowhere else, the opportunity was completely unhampered. conditions and forces which had to be reck oned with elsewhere did nos exist, had not grown up. The nills, which in other places have steadily thwarted expansion, were only sandy undulations. More than that, the industrial union movement, as laid down in the program of the Industrial Workers of the World, not only proposes to organise the steel industry throughout ail its ramifications from the men who mine the iron ore in Minnesota to the workers in the mills but it also proposes to organize at the same time all other in dustries on similar lines. Therein lies the strength; of the irresistible power of the I. W. W. program. All previous industrial union movements have been partial movements only. The Industrial Workers of the World is the first conscious and clear headed attempt to organize the entire working class on the in dustrial field. The preamble to the I. W. W. constitution declares that the working class must be organized in such a way that "all workers in any one industry or in all industries, if necessary, cease work whenever a strike or lockout is on in any department thereof, thus making an injury to one the concern of all workers." Organized in that way, the workers will be enabled to take care of all matters relating to the wages, hours of toil and shop conditions. And as the organization developes, its ultimate goal will appear clearer to the workers. That goal is the ownership and control by the industrial union of all the workshops of the nation. That means the abolition of the employing class and the substitution of the industrial commonwealth for the present system of industry a commonwealth in which every worker will have a share in the ownership and a voice in the control of industry. The industrial union is not confined to the United States. In all other capitalist nations similar movements are now shaping themselves, and will soon unite into a world-wide international-THE INDUS- TRIAL WORKERS OF THE WORLD. A. Goff. After many years practical experience as an iron and steel worker, I regard the pace and speeding up process of the present as the most heart-breaking factor in the workers' lives. To give a full and de tailed history of the speeding up processes of the last 40 years would require too much space in this special edition of Solidarity. But the significance of this pernicious practice ean hardly be over estimated. For that reason, we earnestly advise all of the working class to bethink themselves and take careful note of the speeding up processes and practices. Doing this, they will quickly discover that a world of abuses and misery falling to the lot of the workers are directly chargeable to the killing pace, not alone in iron and steel works, but also in all manner of production, agriculture included. As a matter of course, the practice of speeding up finds its main cause and inspiration in the insatiable desire of the capitalist for exorbitont profits. But that inspiration cannot be gone into in this brief article. We simply state the obvious fact. Where the Workers Blundered The iron and steel workers have been in the past, and are at present, largely to blame themselves for the present killing pace in the iron and steel industry. They bave always labored under the insane de lusion that a reduction in wages or tonnage rates could always be recouped by a greater output; ignorant of the historical fact that, the more work the workers do, the less they get for doing it. It will not answer to assert that improved machinery, and more advanced and scientific processes, have so multiplied iron and steel production. That is part of the truth, but not all of it. Going back to 1868, we find wages (as measured by tonnage rates) reached high mark. In 1868 the puddlers struck seven months for a tonnage (2,240 pounds) rate of $9. After the scale was signed at $9, B. J. Jones taunted his puddlers by telling them that they could just as easily have gotten $18 per ton. Which illustrates the same ignorance of the "labor market" that prevails even to this day among the workers themselves. The scale of 1869 measured the high mark of war prices" as far as tonnage rates are concerned; and from the expiraof that scale, tonnage rates have tione over tower and lower, and the end is not yet. "It is doub'ful if all the devices yet invented have lightened the day's toil of a the output of pig iron in the United States was a trifle over 17,000,000 tons. In 1906 the output of steel in the United States was over 33,000,000 tons. This much boasted of increase in "business" has cost the workers a frightful price, leaving them today exhausted physically and mentally. To overcome the present conditions in the steel industry is no small undertaking, no child's play. He who minimizes the task is exceedingly foolish. And yet, that is the work that is now looming up. As a matter of sheer self-preservation, the steel workers are organizing in the face of enormous difficulties. Could the steel workers once catch the idea, and without any palaver with the boss about it, cease work at the termina. tion of eight hours, it would largely reveal the enormous possibilities of ONE BIG UNION of iron and steel workers. Telegram to Solidarity) Akron, O., March 12. The class war in Akron is on with a vengeance. The police have thrown off the mask of law and order and have shown themselves in their true colors, hirelings of the master class. On Tuesday, March 11, the strikers were clubbed and shot at because they dared to exercise their constitutional right of free assemblage. In the evening the usual picket line was formed outside of the union hall and proceeded to parade to the southern portion or town where the Goodrich and Firestone factories are situated. The parade was an orderly one, the strikers confining their activities to asking the scabs to join with them in the fight. Things were too tame for the police, do they started to club the strikers. Suddenly the dinner baskets of the scabs began to fly in the air. Scabs ceased to smile. The real strike was on. The police desisted for a time and the strikers reformed their lines. 1500 strong they marched without further disorder back to the city. As the strikers got within a few blocks of the headquarters it began to break up, the strikers leaving for their homes. Less than 40 strikers remained in the line being mostly girls and men carrying flags and banners. Then the most cowardly thing that has happened since the strike started occurred. The police and special deputies formed into double file, 62 of them, with other squads following in autos and just as the strikers were disbanding the parade and the girls and banner carriers were about to turn into the hall the police began to club and beat everyone who was within reach. Girls were caught in the narrow hallway of the hall and clubbed without mercy. Shots were fired at the strikers. One girl named Mary Bryan aged 17 years who formerly worked in the Goodrich mill was beaten over the head and shoulders by a big burly policeman. Several men were beaten so that they required medical attendance. The police were so overcome by the powerlust that they did not stop at beating strikers. Several citizens were also beaten. Result some dissatisfaction on the part of the aforesaid citizens. Fellow Worker Bailey was arrested and clubbed on the way to the police station. Late last night houses of strikers were searched and several strikers arrested.
