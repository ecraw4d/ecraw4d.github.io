TO THE TEXTILE WORKERS. This issue of SOLIDARITY sends greetings and a message of hope and cheer in the name of the I. W. W. to you, the tens of thousands of men, women, and children in the textile mills of America. For many decades---even from the infancy of this republic---your ancestors and predecessors in the cotton and woolen mills of New England have battled against, and have gone down to untimely graves by thousands before, the awful ravages of long hours and ever more intense toil. That same fate is yours today; and, as far as you can see, awaits your children and your children's children. Your grandparents, in the early years of the Nineteenth century toiled at the loom or the spinning machine for fifteen long weary hours a day. You and your children, at the same period of the Twentieth century, find your work day only slightly reduced, to 11 or 12 hours, while the burden of your labor has been greatly added to by the perfection of machinery. A century of progress! In which the mill owners, many of them, have become fabulously rich; have built marvelous mill structures the wonders of the age; have enjoyed all the good things of life, such as beautiful palaces and summer homes to dwell in, the best of food and clothing, education for their children, trips to Europe, and other enjoyments undreamed of by you and yours. And all those good things that your master enjoys have been wrung from your very life's blood. His luxury has meant your poverty. To educate, his children, your children have had to leave school even before the "legal limit of childhood" and take up their mind-stunting and bodywarping toil in the factory. To enable your, master's daughter to buy the love and title of some worthless European count or duke, your daughters have been sub- 'jected to the temptations that ever beset the ill paid and overburdened children of the working class. In order that your master's family might drink in the pure air of the mountain or the seashore, you and your children have been forced to absorb the deadly germs of the great White Plague from the dust of the loom. Any many of you have cried out in your agony: "How long, O Lord, how long! What can we do to be saved from this living death?" You have begged the lawmakers to make. laws against child labor and the long work day. And they have done so. But you found that these laws, miserable makeshifts that they are, were not enforced---that your masters controlled their enforcement and that often through poverty, you yourselves were compelled "break the law" by smuggling your child into the mill before the legal age. Time and again you have gone out on strike, in open rebellion against the ops of your travail and misery, the dawn of a new hope and a new day is bursting upon you at last. It is the Rising Sun of INDUSTRIAL SOLIDARITY! In the name of the Industrial, Workers of the World your fellow wage slaves are calling upon all the textile workers of the nation to unite in ONE BIG UNION! A UNION that aims to gather into its folds every man, woman and child working in the cotton, woolen, flax and silk mills of the country. A UNION that will not stop to beg capitalist law makers for a shorter work day, but will demand and enforce AN EIGHT HOUR DAY all along the line from Maine to Alabama by a general refusal of textile workers to work more than eight hours. A UNION that will not ask for "laws against child labor," but will see to it that the wages of parents are raised sufficiently to enable the children to remain at school and at play until their minds and bodies are sufficiently developed. A UNION that will not have to fight its battles alone, but will be supported in all of its struggles by the workers of all other industries, organized in a similar manner. A UNION whose purpose is not alone to improve conditions in the daily strüggle of its members, but whose eye remains fixed always upon that better day, when the workers of the world shall own and operate the industries of the world for themselves, and not for the parasite masters who now fatten upon their life's blood. That is the message that SOLIDARITY is trying to convey to you in this issue. Heed that message. Convey it to your fellow workers. Unite with them under the banner of the 1. W. W., and your children who come after you will rise up and say: "We honor our fathers and mothers; for they saved us from the hell of capitalism!"" HOW TO JOIN THE 1. W. W. Any wage worker, wishing to become a member of the Industrial Workers of the World, may proceed in the following manner: 1. If you live in a locality where there is a union of your industry or a mixed (recruiting) union already in existence, apply to the secretary of that local union. He will furnish you with an application blank.. containing the Preamble to the I. W W. Constitution, and the two questions which each candidate for admission must answer in the affirmative. The questions are as follows: "Do you agree to abide by the constitution and regulations of this organization?" "Will you diligently study its principles and make yourself acquainted with its purposes?" The initiation fee is fixed by the Local Union, but cannot be more than $5.00 in any instance, and is usually $1.00 or less. The monthly dues cannot exceed $1.00 and are in most locals from 35 to 50 cents. 2. If there is no Local Union of the 1. W. W. in your vicinity, you may become a Member-at-Large by making application to the General Secretary, whose address is given below. You will be required to answer affirmatively, the two above questions, and pay an initiation fee of $2.00. The monthly dues are $1.00 for Members-at-Large. SOLIDARITY MILLS ON SHORT TIME A CLOSE VIEW World Wide Depression in the Textile Of the Big Garment Workers' Strike in Industry---Its Causes and the Remedy. By The Commentator. The present textile depression is world wide. It affects all the leading industrial nations. Months ago, the Brussells Cotton Congress recommended curtailment in production. Last month, the Federation of Master Cotton Spinners of Manchester, England, recommended a continuance of short time running and increased curtailment wherever possible. In this country, curtailment has been the rule since spring. It has taken the form of a four-day working week in some instances. In others, the mills have been run alternate weeks. In still others, they haye closed down for two or more weeks at a time. Both the New England and the Southern mills are affected. The extent of the domestic curtailment is indicated in recent press items. In New Bedford, 25 per cent of the machinery is reported idle. Thirty Fall River mills employing 8,000 operatives were shut down. In New Hampshire 25,000 opera tives are hit. At Manchester, notices were posted in the 17 mills of the Kuoskeag Corporation on August 2, announcing that the entire plant will be shut down; 15,500 workers are affected in this corporation alone. In Maine, at Biddeford, Saco, and other places, over 5.000 are feeling the lack of employment. As for the Southern mills, it is estimated that the curtailment now amounts to from 35 to 50 per cent among cloth mills and from 50 to 65 per cent among yarn mills. A corresponding amount of idleness among the operatives is the result. More operatives, thousands upon thousands of them, are thus added to the list of Northern vie tims of Capitalism's anarchy in textile production. As with cotton, so also with woolen and silks, and other allied industries; they too are curtailing, and are in the throes of "hard times." The mills of the American Woolen Co., better known as the woolen trust, for in stance, have been shut down for weeks at a time; those in Providence, employing 5,000 operatives, are shut down for two weeks. The mills of the same company in other parts of New England have been run on the same basis. The corporations not allied with the trust are operated in a like manner. The Skinner silk mills at Holyoke, Mass., have shut down. In Paterson, N. J., "the silk city," conditions are said by operatives to be worse than they were during the panic of 1907. Silk dealers report business at a standstill. At Taunton, Mass., the Mason Machine works have gone on a schedule of 5 days a week. A falling off in the demand for cotton mill machinery is given as the cause of the curtailment. Thus, the depression makes itself felt in all directions. The cause of the textile depression is variously given: Overproduction, high prices, especially of cotton, said to be due to scarcity and speculation; and conditions in general, which are unpromising and in dicative of panic. Coupled with these is the capitalist necessity for employing labor at a profit; this requires an adjustment of production not to actual human needs but to the credit side of the corporation ledger. As may be readily imagined, the depression bears particularly hard on the operatives. At best their wages afford a bare subsistence. When these stop, no matter how briefly, great suffering results and misery abounds. The mill owners are in a reverse position. Their corporations, especially those in New England, pay enormous dividends, and are considered among the best fields of profitable investment in the country. Depressions may come and go, operatives may suffer and starve, but the high average of dividends keeps right on, as if forever; and ther enjoy the bounties of unpaid labor in princely style. Some of the results of the textile depression are already apparent. It has demonstrated the necessity of greater combination and a cotton cultivation free from speculative influences. Accordingly two more big consolidations are announced, one of yarn, the other of cotton, properties. Both of these properties involve tens of millions of dollars. The cotton combine will not only manufacture and sell cotton goods directly, but it will also cultivate its own raw material on its own and. Combination means expansion and absorption in allied industries.... It is hoped that all the textile workers will see the point. The day is passed when one part of an industry is sufficient unto itself. The whole industry must be organized and concentrated. ORGANIZATION COM. Subscribe for Solidarity today. New York City. By G. H. Perry, New York. The strike of the clothing workers in New York City is still on and has now reached the rioting stage, which means that in a few weeks the strikers will go back to work under the same conditions under which they worked before the strike. The strikers, when first called out displayed a splendid spirit of solidarity, and do yet for that matter, but the absence of real revolutionary tactics, and the usual interference of the craft union officials, have worked their customary havoc. Nearly every man, woman and child working in the industry in New York City was called out and the total number of the strikers was between 70,000 and 80,000. Something like 1,200 employers were affected. At first sight it looked as though the men were going to win a complete victory. But on close investigation one could see the craft union tactics, that were obsolete long before most of the strikers were born, being brought into play with the usual de tonating effects. In the first place the strikers seem to think that the signing of in agreement is of more importance than the shortening of hours and the raising of wages. Before the strike was called the cutters had an agreement with the employers calling for a weekly scale of $21.00. From members of the clothing workers 1 diseovered that there has never been a time when all the members of the organization haye received that amount. Their wages have run from $12.00 to $21.00 per week with an average of $16.00. The demand that the striking cutters are now inaking is for a $26.00 a week scale, and although I have talked with hundreds of the strikers I have not found one who believes that the scale of $26.00 will be paid to all the cutters even if the bosses sign the scale. Instead of demanding that the bosses pay the full amount of the previous scale the strikers will insist on having the $26.00 scale signed, and then when it is signed they will go back for whatever the bosses choose to give them. This demand, which is their principal one, is a typical craft union demand. Then, as was pointed out in a recent number of Solidarity, they are "signing up" with a few of the bosses while the others refuse to sign and the result is that the market of the struck shops is being supplied by those wise bosses who have signed up. It is to sob. Another very interesting phase of the situation, which by the way is very common in A. F. of L. strikes, is the handling of scab goods by union(?) workers. In some of the larger houses they have employed a lot of boys and girls, who are having their vacation from school and are anxious to make a little spending money, to cut out the garments and then they are taken to a shop in some nearby city and GOOD craft union garment workers with GOOD craft union cards in their pockets finish up the work and put it on the market. And all the time the fakirs in New York are cheering up the deluded slaves by saying that one more shop was signed up today and victory is within arm's reach. And even if the bosses would sign up, the strikers acknowledge, that they could not get the scale and would have to work for whatever the boss cared to pay: What in hell is the struggle for anyway? The fakirs have turned a good working class strike into a society of freaks whose main object seems to be to secure a fine collection of autographs of employers (sig natures to contracts.) PREAMBLE The working class and the employing class have nothing in common. There can be no peace so long as hunger and want are found among millions of working people and the few, who make up the employing class, have all the good things of life. Between these two classes a struggle must go on until the workers of the world organize as a class, take possession of the earth and the machinery of production, and abolish the wage system. We find that the centering of the management of industries into fewer and fewer hands makes the trade unions unable to cope with the ever-growing power of the employing class. The trade unions foster a state of affairs which allows one set of workers to be pitted against another set of workers in the same industry, thereby helping defeat one another in wage wars. Moreover, the trade unions aid the employing class to mislead the workers into the belief that the working class have interests in common with their employers. These conditions can be changed and the interest of the working class upheld only by an organization formed in such a way that all its members in any one industry, or in all industries if necessary, cease work whenever a strike or lockout is on in any department thereof, thus making an injury to one an injury to all. Instead of the conservative motto, "A fair day's wages for a fair day's work," we must inscribe on our banner the revolutionary watchword, "Abolition of the wage system." It is the historic mission of the working class to do away with capitalism. The army of production must be organized, not only for the every-day struggle with capitalists, but also to carry on production when capitalism shall have been overthrown, By organizing industrially we are forming the structure of the new society within the shell of the old. Knowing, therefore, that such an organization is absolutely necessary for our emancipation we unite under the following constitution. The same night a bunch of workers employed in office buildings, mostly window cleaners, got together and decided to apply for a charter, which was done. The new organization starts of with 27 charter members. This, together with the following, make five locals in good standing in Providence: Textile Workers No. 530; Mixed Local No. 121, Clothing Workers No. 151. Bakers No. 406. Let the good work go on. F.
