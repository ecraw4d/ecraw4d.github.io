Meanwhile, members of this ruling class were immune from punishment, except occasionally through assassination by members of their own class, as in the case of Julius Caesar, for instance. Such acts were in no way related to the class struggle. In feudal times, there were recorded instances where masters, returning cold and wet from the chase, warmed their feet in the guts of a freshly-killed self. Whether such was a general custom or not, in no wise altered the fact that the feudal lord was "legally" immune from punishment for anything he might choose to do to his underling, the serf. For, did not he (the master) himself make the law, and act as judge, jury and executioner, in the courts of the middle ages? The same "legal" principle holds good today. It is less direct, more hypocritical and, therefore, more cunning though no less dangerous in its application. Not only is this seen in the case of the poor chauffeur mentioned above, who gets 30 days for a much less serious offense than that of the society woman, who is compelled nominally to serve seven days only; but is shown much more conclusively in the many strike cases that have littered the courts of this country the past quarter of a century. How utterly amiss, for instance, does it not seem to the really judicial mind, to permit a corporation like the steel trust to hire irresponsible gunmen, allow them to shoot strikers, and then arrest the strike leaders for the murder?
