A sensation was sprung at the Ettor hearing Tuesday afternoon when Greta Zurwell, a 16 year old girl striker, testified that she saw a policeman fire the shot that killed Anna LePizza. "I saw Policeman Oscar Benoit take his revolver from his pocket," swore the witness, "aim it, and then deliberately fire in the direction of the crowd. I saw Anna LePizza drop. Policeman Benoit shot the gun. There was only one shot fired at the time. He aimed the gun at me. I saw the flash, I heard the report and saw the girl fall. There is no question but that he is the man." The Zurwell girl's testimony was in part corroborated by Julia Baluzubick, who said she saw at least one policeman fire his revolver and that a second after one of them had fired, she saw Anna LePizza, who was standing near, pitch to the ground. FINANCIAL CRISES The subject of financial crises is one of interest to all the members of the working class; especially at this time when they are in the midst of one of the severest of them. On this account, the following notes of a recent lecture by Lucien Sanial, delivered in Brooklyn, N. Y., will be appreciated by the readers of Solidarity. According to Sanial, from time to time a great crisis comes on the country. It takes everyone by surprise and few if any are able to account for it. In 1907, there came a panic so suddenly as to startle even the well informed and to render them unable to assign a reason for its origin. What happened in 1907, was not a panic, properly speaking, but the beginning of a prolonged crisis. The latter is not over; in. fact, according to statistical information at hand, we may expect another and much more severe panic, and that very soon. Continuing Sanial pointed out the necessity for a study of the causes of crises and the formulation of new laws concerning them. The old laws are no longer applicable, as the evolution of the capitalist system requires their constant modification. He, through his many years of consideration of the subject, was enabled to make such modification. Crises undergo changes in their characteristics that correspond with the changes in the characteristies of the capitalist sys- Under competition, crises are first industrial and commercial. and financial last. Under concentration, they are first financial and industriel and commercial last. Under competition, overproduction is the rule, owing to the planlessness of production. Under concentration, production is adjusted to the purchasing power of the, various classes. Overproduction exists at no time, when we consider the consumptive capacity of the classes, especially the creators of wealth; who are robbed of the product of their labor. Underconsumption more accurately describes the real condi tion of affairs. Sanial described the purchasing power of the classes under competition, showing that it was an unknown quantity, for the exploitation of which competitor struggled with competitor, with the result that overproduction ensued. This was the period preeeding the Civil war, with its partnerships, etc. About 1860 the intermediary period set in, developing the corporation, pool and syndicate, which made fegulation more possible. The trusts followed, so that now there is complete concentration Concentration created a state of affairs in which the capitalist class was able to adjust production in accordance with the purchasing power of all the classes, not only in quantity, but also in the kinds of com modities specifically desired. Thus the old style crisis is eliminated. During the foregoing description, Sanial dwelt on the crises which occurred during the years embraced by it; also on the great increase in national, that is capitalist, wealth which took place despite them In 1873, a great crisis took place, rendering thousands upon thousands unemployed and causing widespread misery among the working class. Despite this, in 1880, the socalléd national wealth had increased to 40 billions of dollars. Since then it has increased still more, until now it is esti mated at 130 billions; this too notwith standing the crises of 1884, 1893 and 1907. In the crisis of 1878, the financial aspeet came to the fore in the demands of the Greenback movement. This was an attempt on the part of the debtor class to pay the creditor class one-half dollar for every dollar loaned, while the creditor class was determined to exact two dollars for every dollar loaned. In 1893, the increase in the so-called national wealth had been so stupendous, that this country no longer had merely the germs of a plutoc racy, but a plutocracy that was well established. In this panic, the free silver movement played an important part. This was another attempt to pay loans with cheap money. Referring to the charge that the plutocracy had conspired to create the panic of 1893, in order to defeat free ilver, Sanial held such a conspiracy to be unnecessary. He declared that well-developed classes aet consciously and spon taneously in the defense of their interests whenever, those interests are assailed. The Era of Concentration, In 1900, the period of concentration et in. The Philippine war was ended. At gave American capitalism great impetus, securing for it markets abroad, and making it a world-power, with a balance of trade reaching the then enormous figures of $600,000,000 a year. This conferred an immense power on the capitalists of this country, who now dreamt of Wall Street as the leading financial center, and of the United States as the leading financial nation, of the world. This success was achieved, and this dream was to be realized, by adherence to a three-fold policy, which would make it possible for the plutocracy to smash all opposition, whether domestic or foreign. The first phase of this policy was trustification, which aimed to bring all the rail road, industrial and financial corporations under one central ownership and control. The first fruit of this policy was the steel trust, with its one and one-half billions of capital, the most stupendous combinations of capital ever organized. This was followed by other consolidations, so that in 1905 in 37 of the greatest industries there were 34,000 establishments less than in 1900, On the other hand, there had been an increase of capital of over 260 per cent, and an increase in the working class employed of 60 per cent. Added to this, there was five times as much production as in 1880. The second phase was the maintenance of superiority of industrial plants. Old and outworn machinery was discarded; even new machinery was thrown on the scrap-heap, when improvements and new inventions were found more profitable. The result is indicated in the figures deal ing with trustification. The third phase was the reduction of labor to a state of ab solute subjection. Of this, more further on. The period following 1900 was a period of "prosperity"; it gave a new lease of life to the middle class. Bank "deposits increased from five billion. in 1897 to thirteen billions in 1907. Banks, according to Sanial, are divided into two classes, national banks and savings banks, loan and trust companies, etc. The national banka are the property of the great financiers. Their power gives them power over all the banks. They used this power to invest 9 and a half billions or 72 per cent of all the deposits in the watered stocks of the trustified industries. The result is that the banks are left without sufficient reserves wherewith to liquidate out standing indebtedness, In the panic of 1907, there was one dollar of cash to 23 of deposit, in the reserves of the saving banks; and one dollar of cash to 6 of deposit in the reserves of the national banks. Since one dollar in cash will not pay for 23 on deposit, trouble arises when the 25 are required. Saniai declared that the present status of bank réserves is even worse than in 1907; that as a consequence the outlook is not of the brightest, in an economic sense. The Increased Cost of Living. Touching on the third phase of the plutocratic policy, Sanial expressed the belief that it will not be realized without a fight between capital and labor greater than any we have ever seen heretofore. He de clared that there would be an attempt to reduce wages and treak up labor organizations; in fact, such an attempt was now well under way. In this connection, San ial touched on the continuous rise in the cost of living; which he held was another way of reducing wages by reducing their purchasing power. He believed that the increased cost of living was due to the decreased cost of gold production, which tended to appreciate prices. According to the secretary of state of Colorado, the cost of gold production is not one-half of what it was in 1897. Formerly ore assay ing at $8.00 a ton was thrown on the dumps; now it is refined at a profit of 50 per cent. Improved processes of mining and smelting were the cause of this revolution. Never again will prices be the same; prices will increase as gold cost de creases. In conclusion, Sanial looked for a collapse of the gold standard through the reduction of the cost of gold production. In the troublous times that would ensue, it will be necessary, he declared, to convert international socialism into an active force for the beneht of the working class; heretofore it has only been an aspiration. An extraordinary congress of the Danish trade unions was held Jan. 25 at Copenhagen, at which 175 delegates were pres ent. The national trade councils of Sweden, Norway and Finland sent delegates. In the Danish labor world the congress was awaited with the greatest interest, in view of the confusion which is reigning in the movement During the past year several unions such as the masons and seamen left the national trade council. In some other unions the question of leaving the trades council has been fully discussed and occasioned severe attacks on the national trade council and its centralist, tactics. Another important point of the program was the revolutionary syndicalist tactics and in connection with it the strike of the navvies which caused much of the difficulty to the strongest Danish trade union, the "Dansk Arbejdsmindsforbund." We remind our reader that recently 3,000 navvies were on strike at Copenhagen. The leaders of the movement had concluded a contract with the employers, but the men under the influence of revolutionary syndicalist propaganda would not submit, and in a resolution blamed the attitude of the leaders and appointed a new executive. This will explain why the congress was awaited with so much interest. The program was: 1. The situation in the affiliated unions. 2. Form of organization and tactics of the national trade council.
