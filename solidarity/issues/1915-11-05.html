He said (quoting from memory): "There is just one test I will give you; a test you may apply for yourself before election day, if you see fit. You have a friend who desires your written recommendation for a job. Write it out; praise him in the most etxravagant terms you can think of; point out his qualifications for the position; but add three words, in red ink: 'and he drinks.' Then watch the result." SOLIDARITY, CLEVELAND, OHIO Present Day Outlook For Labor Taking a view of the life of the toilers, past and present, we find it one of pain and struggle for the material things of life. The greater the productive power of the workers, the more intense the struggle for existence. Attempts made by workers to improve their conditions are met with one/argument that of force. We are learning that all institutions, whether of church or state, rest upon material interest, and out of this grow the religious and ethical standards. And in this also lies the basis of all progress, not in those socalled moral institutions which would have us go through the realms of the unknown for our salvation. Rather is this last a power for evil, as it deadens the spirit of resistance against wrong and oppression, justifying exploitation, making it possible for the ruling class to exploit the toiling millions of earth with greater security. These suave gentlemen of the robe have their prototype in the politician. One would teach us to be thankful for the things we haven't got, and not to hanker after the good things of life; to be content in that station in which it has pleased an all-wise Providence to place us. The politician plays the part of our earthly Moses. He would lead us out of the Wilderness; all we have to do is to deposit a piece of paper in a ballot box sending him and kind to the pie counter-presto, we are saved. But, seeing both of our saviours are over-anxious for their reward here and now, we take it for granted they are not expecting much above. These two delusions have polluted and chloroformed the mind workers, destroying self-reliance, and making of them dependents and an easy prey for the vultures who fatten upon their misery. It should be evident to any thinking mind, that economic power is all powerful; political but its reflex. The economical ly strong control the state, determine its laws, and ignore them when economic interest demands it. Virtually, there are two governments. One is the direct government, that of shop, mine and factory. The other is the civil government, where the property-holding class adjust their property group interests, and regulate the conduct of the workers outside the industry. Government is based upon vested right in the interest of property-holders as against the interest of the propertyless working class. Acts committed by workers that tend to destroy profit or power of the employing class, are illegal, whether written upon the statute books or not. Through their control of the press and the state, their hired agents are in position to frame up a case against any militant worker in labor's ranks, go through with a farce of a trial, and with a smile upon their lips send their victim to the gallows, all in the name of Justice, Law and Order. What mockery! Is this not evident in the cases of Ford and Suhr, Rangel and Cline, John Lawson, Joe Hill and many others. None of these crimes by the state could be pulled off were the worker's true to themselves and their interests. Where the powers that be have failed to convict the object of their wrath, an employers' movement, designed with an eye for more profits it was not because of any ideal of justice or law, but largely through greater physical efficiency and endurance on the part of through fear of the result of their criminal act upon themselves. the slaves. Whether wittingly or unwittingly Bryan laid bare in that simple illustration the real motive force behind the prohibition movement. The bulk of threshing yet to be done is at Straw and Buffalo. While harvest starts about August 1, and will end about Novembet 1, the rhan who starts in at the beginning and works steadily will get about 50 days' work. Wages this season were $2.50 per day and board; $5 per day for man and team. With the exception of a few ranchers who settled here in the early days and were engaged in sheep or cattle raising, but have since gone to raising wheat, the population consists mainly of renegades from the East. The few old cattle and sheep men stole the bulk of the land in the Basin in order to get sufficient range for their cattle and sheep. In fact, there has been no part of the United States, except here in the Judith Basin, where land stealing was looked upon as a legitimate business. One farmer tells of the methods used by his neighbor in defrauding the U. S. government out of land. And were you to ask one of these old cattle kings about how he acquired his land, he would tell you that he stole it. The homesteader comes to settle up the foot-hills, the rough and rolling lands along the Missouri river, and the "Bad Lands." After living all winter and the greater part of the summer on his homestead, eating "sage hens" and "jack rabbits," and drinking alkali water, he is glad to get down into the harvest fields where he will at least have a change in diet and a drink of water. The homesteader can be easily distinguished from other. men by his lean and hungry look. Capitalism has driven these people around from place to place; in some instances blacklisted them for being active in labor disputes. They hear of a place where they can get a homeatead. They decide to make their last stand in a new country. They think they are going to escape the agony of civilization by making their retreat into the back woods. But usually, after the homesteader has proved up on his land the loan shark is there with a mortgage, or loan contract, ready to demand payment. These vultures, the loan sharks, will follow a workingman into the wilds of Africa. There is no escape from such vermin, except by their overthrow. The homesteader is not a force for higher wages, because he sees his own little farm developing. He sees himself out cutting sage-brush, or digging stones, or breaking sod from early morning until late at night. Thus his well-developed imagination tells him that some day he, too, will be an employer of labor. Sure, he would like higher wages now while he is working, but as soon as he wants to hire a man he reasons that wages are altogether too high. Instead of dealing with conditions that surround his life now, and trying to get wages up as high as possible, he tries to live in the future. The future may never come to him. The Judith Basin should be admitted to I W W territory as soon as possible. Just now the hold-up, the gambler and the bootlegger are conspicuous by their absence. Of course we notice the "jungle drunks" and fights of the "bar-dogs" resulting from these drunks. These bar-dogs are usually conspicuous by their black eye and scratched up faces, yet they seem to be the farmer's choice. If a man is clean and sober he is under suspision of knowing something. From time to time one of their organizers drops into Cleveland and pays us a visit. Four different ones have been here within the past year. These or ganizers visit the various cities. where Hungarian-speaking workers to be found; solicit subscriptions for "A Bermunkas." the J. W. W. in New York, hold paper printed meetings and organize branches of the Hungarian Propaganda League. One of these organizers informed Solidarity recently, that during a tour of ten weeks he sent in an average of $24 a week in subs to "A Bermunkas." Another one has just returned from an eleven weeks' trip, under less favorable circumstanices, with some 160 subs. Several organizations have been formed. These organizers are not paid: they work at their trades during the busy season at pretty good wages, and take a "vacation organizing trip" at their own expense. Thev always maintain a "good front"; are well-posted on the I. W. W. and the American labor movement; and have no time for anuthing else except to promote the work of education and organization.
