The question is often asked: What is Industrial Unionism? The answer is easy. Industrial Unionism is unionism according to industry instead of crafts, for the twofold purpose, first, of improving the present conditions of labor, second, of abolishing the wage system. Industrial Unionism aims to organize labor power in the pres ent and the future interests of the working class. While the old or craft form of union Ais conservative, the industrial unjon is, like industry itself, revolutionary. Instead, of organizing a few skilled trades, Industrial Unionism organizes all the occupations In one given industry, whether skilled or unskilled. Co-operation, not competion, is the principle underlying the new unionism. It believes this principle will extend to inelude the co-operative ownership of capital; and it aims to fit the working class for the new era which this extension will usher in. THE STRIKE BREAKER. As a factor in industrialism the strike breaker never amounted to much at any time; and, at present, he figures up less than he ever did. The strike breaker is essentially the product of competitive wage labor. At best, and most, he is but a temporary expedient, a makeshift, a means to an end: but not the end itself. The chief end of the capitalist labor sweater is, to get the best workman for the least money; and that sort of workers they want to keep in their empley indefinitely. However, there was a time when the strike breaker was, in occasional instances, somewhat of a factor. But this was only rarely, and only as a local factor, where some old style independent" shop was able to use strike breakers to defeat regular employes. But even in such cases the strike breaker was but a mere atom in industrialism, and by no means a factor that need be taken seriously. Now, with the era of trustification in full swing, the small "independent" shop is gone; and industries that are of any real consequence are immense plants, wherein workers are employed in great industrial armies. And these industrial armies are officered, trained and equipped just as systematically as any military army can be. The worker becomes a cog, a lever, or a wheel in an immense automatie process. As a rule skill is but little needed, and therefore does not count for much. The chief requisite is automatie obedience, and automatic action. Every worker must move as every other worker moves; they form a chain of hands, through which the product passes from start to finish; from raw materials to finest product. This process the strike Breaker does not understand; neither does/ he care to. He comes from an element in capitalist socie ty that has never formed habits of systematic industry. They are largely "floaters" who do not want steady employment at anything, at any price. They are of an element that prefers to pick up a precarious living by the exereise of a low type of wit. Their tastes and instinets are more predatory than industrious. If they ever had the work habit they have gotten away from it. This element do the capitalists' dirty work, such as strike breaking, acting as "guards" or deputy sheriffs, repeating at the polls and ballot box stuffing eter, along with an occasional petty theft, or some crime for 'which they are paid a price. Manifest it is clear that great industrial plants cannot be successfully operated with sach Habor. Hence its singulare ten rid of. As to his cost he is at once the most useless, and most expensive nuisance that ever happened. It is doubtful if in any great strike in recent years, that the strike breakers have produced enough to pay for their "grub." As for instance, at McKees Rocks, the strike breakers had a pretty fair tryout; yet, while that strike cost the car company $3,000,000, and eost Allegheny county $1,000,000 more, yet the strike breaker never made a single car; they could not. The real mission of the strike breaker is to stir up trouble, make riots and "outrages" to order; thus creating the pretexts for police and military interence. The really dangerous strike breakers are the men who carry the cards of craft unions in their pockets, and sign sacred "contracts," binding themselves to continue work while other workers strike, union men who haul strike breakers, State constables, deputy sheriffs and State militia, to overawe and shoot men on strike. All the dismal, disgraceful history of craft unionism records the fact that no strike could be broken were it not for the tacit or active opposition of so-called craft unions, whose proper title is organized scabbery. STIRTON, The story goes that in a certain prison the keeper summoned a blacksmith who was one of the inmates and commanded him to make a chain so perfect that human ingenuity could not break it, and that would be impervious to file or saw. Day after day the blacksmith toiled at the forge and with pride and satisfaction beheld his work grow until the chain was of the required length and perfect in workthanship Now, said the keeper, "make manacles of like perfection and attach them to the chain. They are intended for a prisoner especially feared and dangerous to the State." Spurred by the hope that he would be rewarded for his pains and perhaps receive his liberty the blacksmith again took up the task until the chain, nanacles and all, was brought before the keeper and pronounced complete. "Now," said the keeper, "I am commanded to bind you with it." "What! Not to bind me with the chain that I have made myself!" "Such are my orders." "And is this, and this only, to be the reward of my pains and toil? Is it for this that I have worked patiently and long only to create the means to make my imprisonment the more hopeless and harder and harder to bear Would that I had shattered it into a thousand pieces." So is labor today bound the more securely by a chain of its own making, and, as in the case of the blacksmith, made by unpaid iabor. The instruments of production in private hands, with their constant and growing perfection, enable the masters to get the work done with fewer hands, and by so much rivet the chains of slavery more firmly on the wrists of those who are employ- And, as in the case of the blacksmith with the chain, so also is the labor that creates the mills aud factories and all the instruments of production unpaid. The laborer goes to his work and in the first two hours creates the value of his day's wages. The rest of his working hours he must give to the boss. So surplus value is created. The bosses take the surplus value that the laborer creates and divert a part of it to enlarging and perfecting the machinery, the ownership of which gives them power over the lives of those who made them. For all this labor power is like the blacksmith in making his chain, unpaid. Labor produces all wealth. The instruments of production, now in the hands of the master class, simply represerft unpaid wages which have been witheld from the workers by the masters. But here the resemblance of the worker to the blacksmith in our story ends. For him there was no alternative. His making of the chain meant for him simply a more firm and lasting bondage, and it could mean nothing else For labor it is different. The surplus values which labor ereates, and part of which now appear as improved means of production, contam also the promise of labor's emancipation. near! PREAMBLE The working class and the employing class have nothing in common. There can be no peace so long as hunger and want are found among millions of working people and the few, who make up the employing class, have all the good things of life. Between these two classes a struggle must go on until the workers of the world organize as a class, take possession of the earth and the machinery of production, and abolish the wage system. We find that the centering of the management of industries into fewer and fewer hands makes the trade unions unable to cope with the ever-growing power of the employing class. The trade unions foster a state of affairs which allows one set of workers to be pitted against another set of workers in the same industry, thereby helping defeat one another in wage wars. Moreover, the trade unions aid the employing class to mislead the workers into the belief that the working class have interests in common with their employers. These conditions can be changed and the interest of the working class upheld only by an organization formed in such a way that all its members in any one industry, or in all industries if necessary, cease work whenever a strike or lockout is on in any department thereof, thus making an injury to one an injury to all. Instead of the conservative motto, "A fair day's wages for a fair day's work," we must inscribe on our banner the revolutionary watchword, "Abolition of the wage system. The coming convention of the Industrial Workers of the World, which will open in Chicago on May Ist, has a great opportunity before it, demanding the greatest ability and foresight. In all parts of the country there is great industrial nnrest. The cost of living is making a raise in wages imperative; revolt is in the air. The A. F. of L. has proven itself incom petent to either direct or smother the same. Its strikes at Philadelphia and Bethlehem have ended in defeat; its miners' organization is now in a situation that will have the same outcome; its "attacks" on the steel trust is lead by a legal adviser whose failure to destroy the Standard Oil Co. in Ohio is not without its value in foretelling the result of his present endeavors; in brief, the A. F. of L. has failure marked all over it. Nevertheless, such is the pressure of the increased cost of living upon the industrial situation that the capitalists class feel compelled to make wage, increases, notably in railroading and ore-mining, in order to head off revolt. Their efforts will not prove lasting The cost of living, according to all the present indications, viz., the inereasing productivity of gold and the comparative decreasing productivity of agriculture, will continue to mount higher; so also will the industrial revolt. Greater industrial organization will then be needed to secure greater results to the working class. Here then is the opportunity offered to the coming convention of the 1. W. W. Will it rise equal to or superior to it? Will it mainly devote its time to tinkering the constitution, or to mapping a great plan of organization? Constitution tinkering has never cet organized or emancipated a class. The time, energy and money spent in wrestling with the details of a constitution are lost in the midst of epoch-making periods; and we are now there. The first aim of the I. W. W. convention should be to rally around its standard all the real industrial union elements of the country, whether organized or ünorganized. These should be invited to enter into affili ation or co-operation with the I. W. W., with the end in view of considering and promoting ways and means of advancing the cause of industrial unionism throughout the land. Such an aim cannot fail to awake a favorable response, for with.the great industrial unrest there goes the des velopment of a great industrial union sentiment. In the organization of this sentiment the I. W. W. should logically be first and foremost, though neither exclusive nor domineering The second aim of the I. W. W. convention should be the upbuilding of the I. W. W. press. This should include both the increase of circulation and quality. Our press should be more abundantly read: and to this end it must be mostly concerned with current working class doings, rather. than with abstract theory; this sympathetically wherever and whenever possible, but always boldly and truthfully in the interest of the working class. The L. W. w. convention should consider ways and means of making the I. W. W. press more completely the herald and champion of real working class endeavor all over the land. To this end the industrial unions should be requested to furnish locals items of interest; not only of strikes, but of oppressive condi tions that are imposed upon the unorganized; in fact, items of labor's doings and conditions in all their various phases. With a press of this character, the. W. W. will force recognition and make beadway. These, then, are but two of many sugges tions that may be inade to the coming I. W. Wi convention. THE STRIKE BREAKER. As a factor in industrialism the strike breaker never amounted to much at any time; and, at present, he figures up less than he ever did. The strike breaker is essentially the product of competitive wage labor. At best, and most, he is but a temporary expedient, a makeshift, a means to an end: but not the end itself. The chief end of the capitalist labor sweater is, to get the best workman for the least money; and that sort of workers they want to keep in their empley indefinitely. However, there was a time when the strike breaker was, in occasional instances, somewhat of a factor. But this was only rarely, and only as a local factor, where some old style independent" shop was able to use strike breakers to defeat regular employes. But even in such cases the strike breaker was but a mere atom in industrialism, and by no means a factor that need be taken seriously. Now, with the era of trustification in full swing, the small "independent" shop is gone; and industries that are of any real consequence are immense plants, wherein workers are employed in great industrial armies. And these industrial armies are officered, trained and equipped just as systematically as any military army can be. The worker becomes a cog, a lever, or a wheel in an immense automatie process. As a rule skill is but little needed, and therefore does not count for much. The chief requisite is automatie obedience, and automatic action. Every worker must move as every other worker moves; they form a chain of hands, through which the product passes from start to finish; from raw materials to finest product. This process the strike Breaker does not understand; neither does/ he care to. He comes from an element in capitalist socie ty that has never formed habits of systematic industry. They are largely "floaters" who do not want steady employment at anything, at any price. They are of an element that prefers to pick up a precarious living by the exereise of a low type of wit. Their tastes and instinets are more predatory than industrious. If they ever had the work habit they have gotten away from it. This element do the capitalists' dirty work, such as strike breaking, acting as "guards" or deputy sheriffs, repeating at the polls and ballot box stuffing eter, along with an occasional petty theft, or some crime for 'which they are paid a price. ten rid of. As to his cost he is at once the most useless, and most expensive nuisance that ever happened. It is doubtful if in any great strike in recent years, that the strike breakers have produced enough to pay for their "grub." As for instance, at McKees Rocks, the strike breakers had a pretty fair tryout; yet, while that strike cost the car company $3,000,000, and eost Allegheny county $1,000,000 more, yet the strike breaker never made a single car; they could not. STIRTON, The story goes that in a certain prison the keeper summoned a blacksmith who was one of the inmates and commanded him to make a chain so perfect that human ingenuity could not break it, and that would be impervious to file or saw. Day after day the blacksmith toiled at the forge and with pride and satisfaction beheld his work grow until the chain was of the required length and perfect in workthanship Now, said the keeper, "make manacles of like perfection and attach them to the chain. They are intended for a prisoner especially feared and dangerous to the State." Spurred by the hope that he would be rewarded for his pains and perhaps receive his liberty the blacksmith again took up the task until the chain, manacles and all, was brought before the keeper and pronounced complete. "Now," said the keeper, "I am commanded to bind you with it." "What! Not to bind me with the chain that I have made myself!" "Such are my orders." "And is this, and this only, to be the reward of my pains and toil? Is it for this that I have worked patiently and long only to create the means to make my imprisonment the more hopeless and harder and harder to bear Would that I had shattered it into a thousand pieces." So is labor today bound the more securely by a chain of its own making, and, as in the case of the blacksmith, made by unpaid iabor. The instruments of production in private hands, with their constant and growing perfection, enable the masters to get the work done with fewer hands, and by so much rivet the chains of slavery more firmly on the wrists of those who are employ- And, as in the case of the blacksmith with the chain, so also is the labor that creates the mills aud factories and all the instruments of production unpaid. The laborer goes to his work and in the first two hours creates the value of his day's wages. The rest of his working hours he must give to the boss. So surplus value is created. The bosses take the surplus value that the laborer creates and divert a-part of it to enlarging and perfecting the machinery, the ownership of which gives them power over the lives of those who made them. For all this labor power is like the blacksmith in making his chain, unpaid. Labor produces all wealth. The instruments of production, now in the hands of the master class, simply represent unpaid wages which have been witheld from the workers by the masters. But here the resemblance of the worker to the blacksmith in our story ends. For him there was no alternative. His making of the chain meant for him simply a more firm and lasting bondage, and it could mean nothing else For labor it is different. PREAMBLE The working class and the employing class have nothing in common. There can be no peace so long as hunger and want are found among millions of working people and the few, who make up the employing class, have all the good things of life. Between these two classes a struggle must go on until the workers of the world organize as a class, take possession of the earth and the machinery of production, and abolish the wage system. We find that the centering of the management of industries into fewer and fewer hands makes the trade unions unable to cope with the ever-growing power of the employing class. The trade unions foster a state of affairs which allows one set of workers to be pitted against another set of workers in the same industry, thereby helping defeat one another in wage wars. Moreover, the trade unions aid the employing class to mislead the workers into the belief that the working class have interests in common with their employers. These conditions can be changed and the interest of the working class upheld only by an organization formed in such a way that all its members in any one industry, or in all industries if necessary, cease work whenever a strike or lockout is on in any department thereof, thus making an injury to one an injury to all. Instead of the conservative motto, "A fair day's wages for a fair day's work," we must inscribe on our banner the revolutionary watchword, "Abolition of the wage system. It is the historic mission of the working class to do away with capitalism. The army of production must be organized, not only for the every-day struggle with capitalists, but also to carry on production when capitalism shall have been overthrown, By organizing industrially we are forming the structure of the new society within the shell of the old. The coming convention of the Industrial Workers of the World, which will open in Chicago on May Ist, has a great opportunity before it, demanding the greatest ability and foresight. In all parts of the country there is great industrial nnrest. The cost of living is making a raise in wages imperative; revolt is in the air. The A. F. of L. has proven itself incom petent to either direct or smother the same. Its strikes at Philadelphia and Bethlehem have ended in defeat; its miners' organization is now in a situation that will have the same outcome; its "attacks" on the steel trust is lead by a legal adviser whose failure to destroy the Standard Oil Co. in Ohio is not without its value in foretelling the result of his present endeavors; in brief, the A. F. of L. has failure marked all over it. Nevertheless, such is the pressure of the increased cost of living upon the industrial situation that the capitalists class feel compelled to make wage, increases, notably in railroading and ore-mining, in order to head off revolt. Their efforts will not prove lasting The cost of living, according to all the present indications, viz., the inereasing productivity of gold and the comparative decreasing productivity of agriculture, will continue to mount higher; so also will the industrial revolt. Greater industrial organization will then be needed to secure greater results to the working class. Here then is the opportunity offered to the coming convention of the 1. W. W. Will it rise equal to or superior to it? Will it mainly devote its time to tinkering the constitution, or to mapping a great plan of organization? Constitution tinkering has never cet organized or emancipated a class. The time, energy and money spent in wrestling with the details of a constitution are lost in the midst of epoch-making periods; and we are now there. The first aim of the I. W. W. convention should be to rally around its standard all the real industrial union elements of the country, whether organized or ünorganized. These should be invited to enter into affili ation or co-operation with the I. W. W., with the end in view of considering and promoting ways and means of advancing the cause of industrial unionism throughout the land. Such an aim cannot fail to awake a favorable response, for with.the great industrial unrest there goes the des velopment of a great industrial union sentiment. In the organization of this sentiment the I. W. W. should logically be first and foremost, though neither exclusive nor domineering The second aim of the I. W. W. convention should be the upbuilding of the I. W. W. press. This should include both the increase of circulation and quality. Our press should be more abundantly read: and to this end it must be mostly concerned with current working class doings, rather. than with abstract theory; this sympathetically wherever and whenever possible, but always boldly and truthfully in the interest of the working class. The L. W. w. convention should consider ways and means of making the I. W. W. press more completely the herald and champion of real working class endeavor all over the land. To this end the industrial unions should be requested to furnish locals items of interest; not only of strikes, but of oppressive condi tions that are imposed upon the unorganized; in fact, items of labor's doings and conditions in all their various phases. With a press of this character, the. W. W. will force recognition and make beadway. These, then, are but two of many sugges tions that may be inade to the coming I. W. Wi convention. They are enough to show that it has a great work before it; besides which internal details are insignificant indeed. We feel confident that the convention will not slight these suggestions, any more than it will attach undue importance to the mending of the constitution."
