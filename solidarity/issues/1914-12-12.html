Edwards Lessons Of The Ohio Miners' Struggle As will be noted elsewhere, in the article reprinted from the Cleveland "Leader" of December 5, the mine owners of the eastern Ohio district announced the day before that date, that all further negotiations between them and the union officials of the U. M. W. A.. were off, and that henceforth the mines of that district would be operated on the "open shop" basis. Whether or not this will prove to have been only a bluff, to get the miners to come to their terms, remains for further developments to determine. But in view of similar developments in British Columbia, Colorado, Arkansas, and other coal mining districts, as well as in the copper sections of Michigan and Montana, it looks like more than the beginning of a well-defined movement of the coal trust and its allies, to crush the existing unions in that industry. In the Ohio district, 15,000 miners have been locked out since April 1. They have endured suffering from hunger and privation similar to that of their brothers in Colorado, though machine guns and evictions have not been as much in evidence. The alleged pretexts for this lockout on the part of the bosses, were the refusal of the union's officials to accept less than 47 cents per ton for mining coal, and the readjustment of mining operations in accord with the "Green anti-screen law," providing for the payment of all coal mined on a mine-run basis. This law was supposed to become effective on May 15 this year. The Ohio operators put up the plea that, in view of this mine-run law, they should not be compelled to pay more for mining coal than is being paid in the competing Pittsburg district. So, we are told, resulted the long deadlock in negotiations, with mines idle and miners starving. As usual in such cases, the rumor is now circulating that this whole performance is the result of a collusion between operators and leading officials of the miners union.
