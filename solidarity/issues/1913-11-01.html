"THE REAL TROUBLE” WITH JOHN GRAHAM BROOKS John Graham Brooks recently published a book entitled "American Syndicalism; The I. W. W." We have read only a few extracts therefrom, which did not show a very comprehensive knowledge of the principles, forms and methods of the I. W. W. However, we are not going to criticize that book, not have to produce the coal, the metals and other products of the mines needed by society. Ditto with all other producing and distributing groups. If any "group" of workers balks at this necessity, the rest of society will impose its will upon that group. Does anyone doubt the power of society to make good? Then study the inter-relations of industry as at present constituted, even, and that doubt will readily be dissolved. Of course there is another incentive to plentiful and efficient production: it will increase the social share of each individual worker. Take for instance, another illustration-the administration of cities, a less simple problem than that of any individual industry. Even today, very little actual administration work is done by "politicians" who are nominally in charge of that function; and where the "politician"does interfere, inefficiency is the result. Under the I. W. W. plan, the cities will be administered by the MUNICIPAL WORKERS themselves. Watering, lighting, paving, cleaning, providing parks, amusements, education and other social requirements, will be done by different groups of municipal workers, their efforts being correlated by whatever central body they may find necessary. The members of that central body will not be "placemen" or "politicins," but TECH- NICAL EXPERTS, trained for that special service. How could cities be better administered? "The Survey," which is constantly prating about "civic consciousness," would do well to consider this proposition. Professor Brooks has failed to discover "the real trouble with the I. W. W." He has simply shown his lack of knowledge and sympathetic understanding of the I. W. W. program. We should not worry about that. Circulation, then, is of primary importance. It should be a substantial circulation as far as possible, that is, a circulation secured by subscription largely. In the West, this may be difficult, owing to the migratory character of large numbers of workers. But in the East, where the workers are more firmly settled, this should be comparitively easy. At every street meeting and at every local meeting, our papers should be advertised and subscriptions solicited. Especially should our press receive our first consideration; and not the press of other labor elements, as is too often the case with some of our membership. Concentrate on our own press and circulation will be forth-coming to a greater extent than has been the case heretofore. Circulation is not the only thing our press needs. Our press needs news of all I. W. W. doings. It needs to bo kept informed of everything that the I. W. W. is doing, wherever it exists. Our press needs news of all the doings of Capital and Labor, as well as the doings of the I. W. W. It needs better reviews of conditions and a more comprehensive consideration of all social questions. In a word, the I. W. W. press needs more reporters and writers; more sources of information and knowledge, more ramifications for propaganda and achievement, despite all obstacles to the contrary. In brief the I. W. W. press needs expansion and enlargement, physically and mentally. As now published, the I. W. W. press is a thing of patches; a very imperfect presentation of modern economic life as it affects the workers. This must be changed Circulation can change it. They don't beg; they don't "whoop it up" for circulation; they are too busy making ends meet and too modest to blow about it. So it's up to the membership to hustle for them. Let's hustle then! Let the slogan be "Boost the I. W. W. press! On to a better circulation and a bigger publication."
