A few days ago there appeared a note in the Chicago Day Book saying: "As to an article published in The Day Book on Saturday, regarding prices, from the years 1855 to 1860, I will say that I have the information from grandmother who is living today, that potatoes in those days were 12 to 15 cents a bushel and not 15 cents a peck. Also best butter sold at 8 cents a pound and chickens at $1.00 a dozen, weighing from 3 to 5 pounds each. Steaks you could buy at 8 cents a pound, best cut. How do you like that, you who now pay $1.00 a bushel for potatoes and from 20 to 30 cents a pound for steak, and chickens-oh, lord! you all know you can't get chickens anyway except through direct action in the jungles, when you are just about starving to death. So there you see, you satisfied man, you who gather the grain for instance, for the "poor farmer." But, did you ever hear a farmer say: "Oh, the poor working man, he needs to make a few cents and I guess we'll get along all right even if we have to pay him a living wage"? Oh, no, none of that goes with him. "I want to get this done as cheaply as possible," says he. "If I can get 'em for $1.50 it will be so much better, and I will try my damnedest to not pay a cent more than I really have to." That's the psychology of the average farmer as well as of allemployers of labor. Why? Because it is to their interest to get everything done as cheaply as possible. The lower the wages. they have to pay (always remember they will never pay a cent more than they really have to) the more automobiles and good times they will have. And the lower the wage you are willing to work for the sooner you will come around, hat in hand, and a starved, pale-blue expression on your countenance, and ask for something to eat. When you have read this once, read it again, and see if the above facts are not true. See if it is not necessary for you to organize and stick together if you want more of what you produce. Always keep in mind that every one of you has as much right to the good things of life as has the parasite who lives off your toil. In fact, he shouldnt' have any such "right," because he has not been working or produced what he "owns." Some workers have produced it for him, and they are thus the only ones who have any moral right to it. Also remember that the only way to get it is to fight for it through organization. Garment Industry Call An American Labor Congress The heading of this article appeared as the headline to an editorial in Solidarity, June 12, written by Covington Hall. Why not? Is it not up to the only international labor organization now in existence to do these things? The old International has gone to smash, so all revolutionists say. Even the "yellow socialists" say we must reorganize the old International. The "reds" affirm this, but say it must be organized on a different basis. They fail to tell us on what basis they would have us organize. If they say they are going to organize on the basis of the "class struggle" and wage the "class war" in the future, they simply admit that the old International was not based upon the class struggle, and, therefore, was not enlisted in the class war. Hence its failure. If we are to reorganize an international labor organization, not based on the class struggle, will we not be wasting our time and energy? The old International has died, and been buried beneath a million carcasses of the working class. Let us forget it. The New International is here. It is based squarely on the class struggle and is destined to wage the class war in the future as it has in the past. If we are to understand a new base to be different tactics, as some socialists seem to carry the idea, then they must mean a change in tactics from "political action" to "direct action." If that be their meaning, then again we of the Industrial Workers of the World insist that there is no need for a new International to be organized; the I. W. W. is the new international that measures up to both of these demands. It is based on the class struggle because it is organizing the workers in industry, and on a purely economic basis with a revolutionary purpose in view. It teaches direct action because direct action is the only logical action for workers, organized on the basis of the class struggle, to take. Direct action is the action of the workers in industry. Political action is the action of representatives of labor outside, and away from, industry. Those who wish to get away from the fight, or take no part in the class conflict, advise political action. Those who advise direct action, advise staying on the job where labor is robbed, and where the class struggle expresses itself most, and fight it out there. Since we have an organization that measures up to the demands of the times, and is capable of meeting the demands of the times in the future as it has in the past; and the times seem to demand such a congress as Covington Hall has mentioned, why, let's call it.
