(Special to Solidarity.) New York, Jan. 17. The International Hotel Workers' Union, an organisation of hotel and restaurant workers with a membership of 25,000, has been on strike in the city of New York for the past week. The strike was called to protest against certain conditions that prevail in this industry. These conditions are: insufficient pay, long hours, poor food, unsanitary conditions where these workers est, where their clothes are stored during work hours, toilet, etc., and the interven tion of the employment shark between the workers and their jobs. The strikers have formulated some of their demands as follows: 1. The abolition of the tipping system for waiters, and the establishment of a fixed weekly wage scale to be not less than twenty dollars ($30) per week; 8. The establishment of an eight-hour day for all workers in the hotel and restaurant industry; 8. The abolition of the practice of en gaging employes for hotels and restaurants through subsidized employment agencies, and the establishment in their place of employment bureaus in which the hotel and restaurant workers shall have equal control with the hotel and restaurant em ployers. The following members of the I. W. W. have been on the job of assisting the L. H. W. U. to win their strike through I. W. W. methods: Gurley Flynn, Carlo Tresca, Joseph Ettor, William Trautmann, W. Egerman and A. Giovannitti. Thus far, their efforts to enthuse a spirit of solidarity amongst the strikers have been crowned with great success. Large mass meetings Ifave been held every evening, and some of the best and most convincing industrial union propaganda that has ever been given out in so short a time in this city has resulted. French, German, Italian, Greek, English and Bohemian speakers have all spoken to the same point, namely: the necessity of an industrial form of organization if the workers would win their battles against the employing class. THE DECLINE OF THE BALLOT The New York World, in an editorial on "The Popular Vote," takes pains to destroy "the common belief that the presidential vote in 1912 was less than the presidential vote in 1908." It declares that this belief is incorrect. It quotes figures from The World Almanac to show that "the total vote this year was 15,058,669, as against 14,888,442 four years ago, an increase of 145,227." As an argument on the actual increase in the popular rote this showing is too literal. It forgets to consider the increase in the voting population from 1908 to 1912. If this is taken into account it will be seen that voting is on the decline, greatly so. According to the World Almanac of 1912 the popular vote in 1904 was 13,510,648; in 1908, 14,888,442; a gain of 1,877,794, or 1,232,564 more than is recorded as the gain of 1912. In this connection, it must be remembered that the voting population from 1908 to 1912 was not only increased by native and naturalized male voters, but also by the extension of woman suffrage. In California, alone, some 400,000 voters were thus added to the list. This, alone, is almost three times the amount of the increase in the popular vote, as given in the World editorial. Right here, we might take another view of the popular vote in its relation, not only to the increase in the number of voters, male and female, but also in its relation to the entire body of voters. The United States census estimated, prior to the last election, that there are about 27,000,000 males of voting age in this country. Taking this as a basis for comparison with the popular vote of 1912 it means that there are over 12,000,000 who do not or can not vote. In other words, nearly 50 per cent of those able to do so do not or can not exercise the right of franchise. If the figures of 1908-1912 indicate anything they indicate an increase in this tendency. Why is this? Why do voters refuse to vote? Are they disfranchised by conditions, or are they convinced that the vote is impotent and are, accordingly, looking for new means to assert their power? CLASS STRUGGLE IN NEW ZEALAND (Special to Solidarity) Auckland, New Zealand, Dec. 12. There have been some exciting events in "God's own country" during the past few weeks, and the class struggle has been manifested pretty plainly. A long stubboro fight has just ended dramatically at Waihi; and the weakness of sectional strikes has again been demonstrated. On May 18th, upwards of 1,600 gold miners and battery hands ceased work. Their union was one of the strongest and most militant groups in the New Zealand Federation of Labor. For months prior to the outbreak repeated attempts had been made to form a seab union. Similar at tempts were being made in various other districts where unions had withdrawn from the arbitration system and obtained better conditions by dealing directly with employers. In terms of Arbitration Act, 15 men can form a union and coerce any number of their fellows to work under conditions arranged between the 15 and the employers through the court unless labor is organized and militant enough to prevent it. The Waihi trouble was the result of a carefully prepared plan of attack on organized labor. About 20 engine drivers had joined their "Arbitration" union at the behest of the bosses. The Waihi union's working agreement was about to terminate; the seab union was to be formed; a large proportion of members of the old union were to be persuaded and coerced into joining; a new award or agreement was to be fixed by the court; sub-contracting was to be re-introduced; the old mil itant union was to be broken up, etc. II successful it was to be done elsewhere; and thus, arbitration, supposed to be optional, was to be made virtually compulsory. The Waihi men were alive to all this and they took prompt action by strik ing. The magnificent solidarity they have since displayed has been a surprise to the mine owners and an object lesson to the working class. The Waihi strike committee proyed that they knew how to conduct a strike; the working class outside of Waihi brought about their defeat. From the start anything approaching violence was carefully avoided; the strikers offered to do their own police work and guarantee no violence; but it was not long before a large force of police were drafted in, who soon showed what they were there for, but failed to provoke violence from the strikers. At the outset, the strikers out numbering the scabs by 20 to 1 could have pulverized the latter, but not a single case of assault could be shown; even the capitalist press had to admit that they were the most orderly strikers in Aus tralasian history. Presently professional strike-breakers and ex-pugs and slam-scum began to drift in. The conference of the Zealand Federation of Labor took place at Wellinton about a tortnight after the strike started. A motion to declare a general strike was defeated, and a further motion to leave the conducting of the strike in the hands of the Waibi strike committee was also lost; some plain talk came from militants at the conference. In the meantime the mine owners were getting desperate. Though the seab union was steadily growing, something had to be done. The jailing of the strikers commenced; several went to jail for refusing to be "bound over to keep the peace" which they had not broken. Then protest meetings were held all over New Zealand. The I. W. W. took the initiative in Auckland and soon had the waterside workers and the militant S. P. members with them. The Auckland waterside workers struck one day on their own hook to meet and cheer a batch of strikers on their way to jail, genuine arbitrationists among them sinking their differences for the time being. The New Zealand Federation of Labor executive got busy collecting funds; two men were sent over to Australia on a tour for the purpose; unions were addressed everywhere by Waibi strikers, militants and federation men, and every effort was made in Auckland by the I. W. W. men and others to keep feeling alive in the working class. Meanwhile the scab union grew-not by miners joining, however and more men went to jail for saying "scab" and for lesser offenses Police and strike-breakers got more aggressive. Then the federation's executive requested all nions to "take a day off to protest. Several unions came out in response for one day only all over the country, but on separate days. Auckland was in the van of the fight outside Waihi; the waterside workers and general laborers, and several odd bunches of men from other unions, together with the I. W. W. and many S P. members, marched in procession headed by the Wathi band to the gates of the jalil at Mount Eden, and afterwards held a big demonstration in a public park. In Waibi matters were getting worse; the seabe and police were now stronger than the strikers, and seabs began to attack isolated miners; the miners were weakened by men leaving the district and men being sent to jail, but those still in Waihi were very determined. The daily press of New Zealand kept up its united howl and its misrepresentations, trying to persuade the public that it was a fight be tween factions of labor, ete. Earlier in the struggle attempts had been made and had failed to divide the men on religion; then "patriotism" was tried. An old ragged Union Jack which a miner had been using to dust a table, was unthink ingly thrown in the garbage pile; the press seized upon this and made great attempts to use the incident to work up sentiment against the strikers, saying they had torn down the union jack and hoisted the red flag. Then the scabs and some of the storekeepers, etc., started wearing miniature flags, and flying them on their houses and premises. A big union jack was kept flying over the office of the seab union. A few stand-up fights took place between strikers and strike-breakers, the strikers always making a ring and seeing fair play. If the striker was winning, the police would ride in and stop the fight. The seabs had been going to and from the mines in conveyances guarded by mounted police. They now discarded the vehicles and went in a procession on foot, carrying their flags with them, and at the end of October they had grown very ag gressive. It was early in November that Waihi qualified to rank with Lawrence and San Diego in infamy. On November 12 the seab procession went out of its way to cross the street near the Union hall. The police, were handy watching. A few strikers and two or three of their wives were in the hall. They had got a hint that trouble was brewing and attempted to close the door. A rush was made and the door pulled open by seabs. In the confusion shots were fired. Police Constable Wade struck Fellow Worker Evans, who was inside the hall, a heavy blow with his baton. Evans fell to the floor and the thugs gathered around and kicked him as he lay insensible. When picked up later he was dead During the subsequent inquiry, Mrs. Sorrenson (wife of a striker) giving evidence, stated that "no shot was fired be fore Wade struck Evans. There was no aggravation of any sort to cause the blow to be delivered. While Evans was on the ground motionless, a seab strpek him with a baton, and other spabs crowded sround him and kicked him." Kathleen Hyslop stated that she saw a policeman run up and hit Evans on the back of the bead after he fell; one of the seabs did the same thing with a baton, After that the mob of seaba got around Evans and appeared to be kicking him. Strong drink was supplied to the seabs, although Waihi is a prohibited district. For two or three days the thugs and seaba were allowed to run riot and take charge of the district; isolated miners were attacked and beaten, women were territied and compelled to remain indoors, the furniture of one striker was piled up outside the house and set afire. One woman gave birth to a still-born child through fright. Militant unionists were given 48 hours' notice to quit the district under threat of death. In short Anarchy and Thuggery were let loose in Waihi; the whole affair brings San Diego to mind. Happening to be working in a railroad junction town where Waihi trains come through, I met and interviewed several. strikers who had just left Waihi on the day of the "riot." They were clean, strong, level-headed men who spoke quietly and without boasting of the fight they had made. They all told a straight and similar story of thuggery and outrage. The strike committee met in Auckland a few days after the disturbance and the strike was declared off November 29. The Auckland S. P. and I. W.
