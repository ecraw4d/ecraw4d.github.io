(Special to Solidarity.) San Diego, Calif., May 14. A police autocracy conducted in the interest of a financial monarch is now an established fact in San Diego. The liberties that buman beings are supposed to enjoy by right of birth do not exist here. Everyone who has ever been known to expressa sentiment of freedom has either been driven from the town, thrown into jail, or is in constant danger of bodily harm at the hands of the police and vigilantes. Anyone who has not been through the expriences of the past two months here, which culminated in the murder of Joseph Mikolasek on the 7th of this month by police officers, can have no idea of the conditions as they really exist A member of the Socialist Party who has recently been in the northern part of the state says that the people outside San Diego cannot possibly have a clear idea of what has taken place here, as they have slmost uniformly claimed that the accounts of police brutality were certainly exaggerated, no matter from what source they came. In this connection I wish to state that the accounts appearing in the I. W. W. and other labor papers are in no wise exaggerated, but that all statements have been well within the truth. Every vicious passion has been let loose in the interest of the Spreckles crowd of thieves, who are the virtual monarchs and actual owners of more than two-thirds of San Diego. No one is safe on the street, and private residences and rooms are be ing broken into daily. The room of C. E.. Payne was entered by a city detective, and some letters from the general secretary found, and the story was at once started that some of the high officials would be connected with the fight here. Also two suit cases that belong to others were taken. The home of a Mr. and Mrs. Bauer was broken into in daylight by the vigilantes, the furniture turned topsyturvy, and Mrs. Bauer, who is in a delicate condition, left in a state of nervous prostration. WHO JOSEPH J. ETTOR IS The name of Joseph J. Ettor is now heard throughout the land. It is on the lips of workingmen and women in the remotest parts of the country. It is indissolubly connected with the great Lawrence strike and the industrial revolt in New England which began there. It is a name that is destined to become famous in conjunction with the attempt of reactionary capitalism to kill revolutionary untonism by means of the infamous legal doctrine of "accessory before the fact to murder"-a doctrine from which even despotic Russia is free. Despite the widespread use and the his torie connection of his name, despite the fame which posterity is likely to bestow upon him, little, very little is known of Joseph J. Ettor, the mane The question is accordingly often asked: Who is Joseph J. Ettor? What is his personality like, anyhow? How did he become so well known? These questions are well put. Any innocent labór leader whom it is desired to put to death in the interests of capitalism and according to its most oppressive legal perversions, is a man of merit and worthy of intimate working class acquaintance. Joseph J. Ettor is a native of the United States. His parents are Italian. He is slightly over 26 years of age, and unmarried. Though short and stocky, he walks with the quick nimble step of a woman. His hair is black and flowing. His eyes are dark brown, his cheeks fat and rosy. His whole manner is open, candid and boyish. His attire, at best, with his big soft hat worn jauntily on one side and his big flowing windsor tie and natty blue suit is suggestive of the prosperous bourgeoise or the artistic Bohemian-though Ettor is neither of these, being sound and substantial in all respects: A Child of the West. Ettor has lived most of his life in the west. In addition to the audacious, quick witted enthusiastic temperament inberited from his Latin ancestors, the west has given him practical fortitude and an indomitable spirit. There is nothing absolutely volatile and effervescent about Ettor. He is a light and gay spirit united with many sturdy qualities, both of head and heart. He is an energetic, bright, courageous young man of ideals.. Ettor's birth place is Brooklyn, N. Y. Few things but churches, grave yards and bed chambers are known Brooklyn. But it has within its confines, some of the biggest industrial trusts and plants in the country, and now it can claim the honor of having been the city in which this modern labor leader first opened his eyes. Ettor was not allowed to enjoy the sight long. His parents left funereal and somnolent, Brooklyn shortly after he was born. They migrated westward. His father, a laborer, was in Chicago during the great eight-hour strikes of 1886. He was in Haymarket square on the night of the bomb throwing. He was struck in the back with a brick during one of the many melees attending this epoch-making period in the history of the American labor movement. The youngster, Joe, as he is fondly called by those nearest to him, ofteti beard his father recount the foregoing incidents. "The old man," as Joe affectionately refers to him, "was proud to be a striker in those stirring times." It was from his father that Joseph J. Ettor first imbibed revolutionary spirit. "COMRADE" FAKIR ON JOB (Special to Solidarity.) Baltimore, Md., May 20. Some five weeks ago local stevedores went out on a strike for higher wages and better conditions. At that time I was billed to fill dates in the East. About 1,000 local tailors expressed a desire to join the I. W. W., and as soon as I was through in the East I stopped here. The stevedores joined the Transport Workers' Federation of America, and are still on strike. A few days after my arrival here a few Russian fellow workers suggested that I say at least a few words of encouragement to the Russian strikers, as I speak that language. I went to the union quarters, introduced myself, and expressed my readiness to render any service at any time they desire it. On the next day they arranged a meeting for me. I spoke to the strikers, and made it plain that although I am not connected with the Transport Workers' Federation, but that the sympathy of I. W. W. is with the fighting workers, I advised them to follow the instructions of their union, fight and stick together. On another occasion I addressed them in English, and again advised standing together and following the policy of their union. I told them the 1. W W. is always in sympathy with those who stay united and fight for their rights, whether they be I. W. W. men or any other revolutionary body. Later, a fellow worker came after me at 6 o'clock in the morning and reported that over 100 men revolted at Curtis Bay, a suburb of Baltimore, and that they wanted me to organise them. I went, but when I learned that they are coal loaders I advised them to join the T. W. U. of A. I personally went to the union quarters and reported the case So you see there was no rivalry contemplated. While organizing the workers at Baltimore into one big union I heard that the copper smelters talk strike, but that they they do not belong to any union, but are ready to join one. Little by little I got the addresses of some smelters. I called on them at their home, and one by one I got them together. It was a tedious and slow job, but I kept at it. My plan is to carry education and organization simultaneously, so at each meeting I discussed the principles and tactics of the class struggle. At the very start, a couple of Social Democrats began to cause trouble because I tried to keep politics out of the union. Behind my back they met and decided to drag in the politicians of the S. P. I kept up the work. We spent time, money and energy, and finally about 100 men joined the union, and 300 smelters attended our meeting. The bosses began to feel uneasy and, hoping to head off the encounter, raised each worker 10c a day. Everything went on lively; our members were enthusiastic. All the preliminary work completed, I arranged a mass meetng for Sunday, May 12. J. P. Thompson came from Philadelphia to address the workers at that meeting. In the meantime the couple of socialists who were determined to act as agents of the American Federation of Lobor went to Lawyer Jackson and they worked out a crafty, brasen plan. Lawver Jackson is an S. P. politician and an A. F. of L. boss. He is acting as sttorney and leader of the striking stevedores. His tricks and chicanery in the S. P. itself are repulsive, so there is not much to be surprised at of his mean ways outside of it. At our meeting on Sunday he came to the hall with his Polish second, Stanishefsey, who is also trying lately to do something, and they mounted the plat form. First, "Comrade" Jackson wished to know who allowed us to have that meeting. It was gently explained to him that as yet he is not chief of police of this town, although he may attain that high rank some of these years if his political methods prove successful. While Fellow Worker Thompson was speaking "Comrade" Jackson got busy. His deputy ran up to the stage several times to receive orders from him. The Jacksons are two brothers. both lawyers. One plays his role as leader and adviser; the other works silently in the ranks and, organises the forces. That method proved useful this time also. Af ter the deputy took orders and the chief himself went down with a few good boys to the back room, the last rows in the back of the hall were filled with faithfuls, some of them not altogether sober, but to perform any bid. All that was conducted brazenly and openly. I told the lawyer then that those are just the methods a dirty democratic politician will resort to at the back room of a saloon before election. "Comrade" Jackson demanded the floor. He jumped up, began to yell and dance on the step, and delivered himself thus: "You know me, all; yes? That manpointing at me called me a politician. Am I a politician?" No, No! hoarsely roared the back rows, you are all right! "Then," continued the chief, "you see I am fighting for the workers; am ready to give up everything for you; am ready to go to prison. We have our union. I want you to join there. You don't need any other. Join the Transport Workers' Federation of America." "Yes! Yes!! Hurray! readily echoed the back rows. "Then," concluded the yeller, "march right out of the hall after me to our quarters. Give three cheers for the T. W. F. of A. and march right after me. Hur- Everything was beautifully arranged. The mob began to whistle and yell and swear. They followed instructions to the limit. And so the meeting was broken up. On the platform were a few members of the S. P. They were shocked and disgusted. They called to the people to remain. They tried to quiet those that yelled and swore I called out loudly to those willing to hear the others tell of the case to remain. About 100 did so. "Comrade" Stanishefsky, the self-appointed aide-de-camp of the chief, undertook to set matters right, because most of the workers left were Polish. He spoke sanely for twent minutes, but then began to blunder, and, when asked to stop, called on the people to join the A. F. of L., and to come to their meeting next Tuesday. That was already too much, even for the few socialists in the bali. We then threw full light on the shameful conspiracy, and the meeting was over. As a score of workers later explained, they even did not understand what "Comrade" Jackson was yelling about. They heard the yell of the chief, and of the back rows, the word "union" and saw men marching out, so they followed, as they always will, as long as ignorance prevails. Now, you know there is a class of people paid by the police to break up meetings of workers. They are called often "provocateurs." This time it was not for the coin, but for the sake of furthering personal political career We know of no greater crime and disgrace in the revolutionary ranks than sesbs and provocateurs. Well, we don't have to wash our hands of this affair; they are clean, but those who did the dirty work will not wash their names of this disgrace. And as it has been perpetrated by a leader and boas of of the S. P., it is up to that body to clean its ranks of such characters, otherwise it tacitly becomes a party to this affair and shares the laurels of the "glory" of such acts. The matter is being discussed lively here, and the more clean and moral members of the local S. P. are disgusted with it and bitterly denounce it. Besides, the question now raised is what have the smelters to do in the T. W. of A? To be consistent, then, take the jewelers into the Garbage Collectors' Union, or the cooks into the Shoe Polish ers' Union of America. But the solution of the riddle is concealed in the fact that "Comrade" Jackson is at the same time boss in the A F. of L., and that is where the smelters are being dragged. Let the reasoning workingman profit by this experience. Stop being such an easy mark for political howlers and refuse to be led by the nose along with your ignorant neighbor. Learn the idea and principles of industrial unionism. JOIN THE ONE BIG UNION-the Industrial Workers of the World, which stands for organization and education-where you are not led, but taught to work for yourself and get what you produce what belongs to you. ACTIVITY IN NEW YORK Solidarity: New York City, May 18. The springtime here in the East seems to wake up the working class a little. Since the battle in Lawrence the workers are beginning to take more interest in the idea of industrial unionism. Also the Jewish mass here in New York is showing signs of life. Since we organized a Jewish local six weeks ago, we could almost say that the membership grows obviously day by day, and we have during that short time done a good deal of useful work. Almost every evening we Lold open-air meetings on different street corners, and they are generally well attended. The workers pay great attention to our speak- We have collected the sum of $:0 for the Ettor and Giovannitti defense fund and our delegate received $50 from the "Arbeiter Ring" convention for the same purpose. This money has been forwarded to Lawrence. To day we called an Ettor- Giovannitti Defense Conference, to be composed of all labor organizations, and arranged for a great prötest demonstration for May 25 at Union Square. POLITICIANS AND WORKERS Solidarity: Indianapolis, Ind., May 10. The Socialist Party convention has advertised the 1. W. W. very extensively. The fight was plainly a contest of sky pilots and politicians for rule of what they are pleased to term OUR movement. Their HORROR was great at the suggestion (which by the way was their own) that the starving masses might not wait for the ballot to make starving illegal. However, the workers were not caught in their snare, but are more than ever determined to get rid of middle class rule and organize a class, even if we should lose the valuable (?) assistance of that element. Haywood is well satisfied with the situstion; says, "We have the workers on our side. He was the most popular man at the convention. When he was away from his desk swarms crowded him and not a moment was his own. I believe those who can should take part in the political movement, as the existence of the movement proves the present neersity of such a movement, and many can act in no other organized form. Edu cation and organization are preliminary steps to co operation, and neither should be neglected. Socialist Party convention is a good thing.
