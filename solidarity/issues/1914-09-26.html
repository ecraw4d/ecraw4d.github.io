WHOLE No. 246 WILSON'S COLORADO TRUCE PLAN ANALYZED It is useless for me to try for a scholastic rendering of my feelings and suspicions after having read over the President's three-year truce plan for settling the difficulties in the Colorado mine war. Events forerunning it have been too strongly engraved on my mind to be blotted out by a sweep of the pen, which could not have been better done had the terms of the plan been dictated by the fuel barons themselves. And the fact that the newspapers of the following day (September 9th) published reports that "It is confidently stated that the President's proposal will be accepted by the men." And that "From statements made thus far by the operators, it is believed they will reject the President's proposition," goes far to convince me that my first theory is correct: that the whole plan has been made at the suggestion, and even most likely, at the dictation of a lawyer hired by the Colorado barons. Tell me, why "It is confidently stated that the men will accept the proposition. Who confidently states such a thing? Haven't the men found that the government operates in favor of the bosses? And, is not the statement made in order to prejudice the men's minds in favor of the plan submitted to them, probably by the bosses themselves, through the intervention of the Government? And why is it stated in the same breath, that the operators will reject the plan? Is this not also an assertion made to pull the wool over the miners' eyes as to the actual origin of the plan? Let us take up the plan item by item and clause by clause. 1. "The enforcement of mining and labor laws stated." This demand is an admission that the laws have not been enforced in the past. It is an admission that the operators have been violators of the law and have gone unpunished. 2.
