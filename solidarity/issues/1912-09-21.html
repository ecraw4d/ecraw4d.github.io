"You can keep the persons of Ettor and Giovannitti confined, but the spirit is free and with .s." As these hosts of the irrepressible spirit of labor, determined to overthrow capitalism, marched through Boston's streets, amid stirring enthusiasm, the natives gaped in wonder; they knew in a dim, imperfect way that the 1. W. W was due in Boston that day, but that it would come from Lawrence in such numbers and with such a spirit had not been expected. Many were the ejaculations of surprise, many, were the comments expressive of appreciation of the significance contained, in this march of Labor's hosts. The po lice made no attempt to constrain the pa raders; they could come and go as they chose. Nor were the trolley employes any te courteous. At all crossings they beld up their cars to permit the passage of the triumphant army, while the occupants craned their neeks and wondered what it all meant. The natives were cer tainly astonished; but their astonishment increased when told another contingent would enter Boston from Lawrence in the afternoon; while Lynn, Haverhill and other cities would contribute their quota to the army of Industrial Workers. Departure From Lawrence. Great as was the entry into Boston, still greater was the departure of the 1. W. W. hosts from Lawrence. A special train of two sections had been chartered to leave Lawrence at 10 a. m. Before that hour the North station was the scene of an assemblage of over 10,000 persons. ZATION In reply to Fellow Worker Biscay I wish to say that decentralization does not mean division when the term is applied to a labor organization, nor does centralization mean unity. If Biscay won't take my word for it, he has only to watch the A. F. of L. anv old time and anywhere, to get all the proof he needs The constitution of the I, W. W. does give the outline of a centralized organization, but the I. W. W. itself is decentralized, and has been so from the first. Except in matters of book-keeping, I have never known the General Office to send out a positive order to the locals: and whenever the General Office did send out anything that could be construed as an order, it has been most conscientiously and universally ignored. If you don't believe me, just ask the General Secretary. This is one rude fact that stares us in the face. Biscay informs us that the organization which oppresses us is highly centralized, and he advises us to organize the same But that capitalistic organization was built up in order to maintain the present system of slavery, and is most beautifully adapted for this purpose We don't want to maintain this systein of slavery, and we don't want any other forin of slavery. Capitalism is centralized because it is maintained by coercion; orders are obeyed because there is the power to compel obedience. WE have no power to compel obedience from our fellow workingmen, and don't want any such power. If we had any such power we would most assuredly misuse it, as everybody else has done who had the chance. We are struggling for Industrial Freedom for the whole working class, and we don't propose to train the workers for that freedom by any method of coercion But it would be foolish" to build a de-centralized organization, because "in that event each local body would represent a wheel which could turn back wards or forward or refuse to turn at all." Suppose the wheel does refuse to turn. What are you going to do about it? Take away the charter? Will that hurt the local body to lose a piece of paper? Or will it help the I. W. W. to lose a local? Or perhaps you would expel the members of that local body from the working class? It is a good deal to expect, I know, but I do wish that the advocates of centralization would try to understand that the 1. W. W has not now, and never will have, any method of coercion that can be used effectively against members of the working class; and for this reason a centralize i organization of the working class ja a pipe dream or a nightmare-whichever you like. As for the relative efficiency of the two fortis of organization, Biscay is as badly at fault there. Capitalists are not afraid of a centralized organization, because it is usually possible to buy some of the officials, and it is always possible to watch the rest If you know a man's mental caliber, you will also know what to expect from him under given conditions. If the officials run the organization, you will always know beforehand what the organization will do. But there is no possible way for the em ployer to estimate the mental caliber of the unknown, individual who may at any time influence the decision of a local union, and the employer has nothing to indicate what this local may do until the decision is made. This isot the greatest advantage to Jabor union-the ability to act without giving the employer any time for preparation-and it is only poss.ble where there is freedom from official coercion. Biscay tells us that decentralization is a source of confusion in the 1. W. W. Sure thing. Also, the collapse of a build ing is at least partly due to the force of gravity; we should therefore change the law of gravitation if we wish to prevent the collapse of a building. But every calamity has more than one cause, and when one cause is discovered, and it is found that there is no remedy for it, it is a good idea to look for some other cause which can be remedied. It is a rude fact that decentralization is a natural and necessary characteristic of a revolutionary working class organizatian, and we must recognize this fact and act accordingly, if we wish to avoid confusion. Education and mutual understanding is the only road to working class solidarity, and the very attempt to seek some easier way can only bring division and confusion, I make no claim that the rank and file is fittest to judge on matters pertaining to the organization, but I insist that the rank and file of the 1. W. W or of the work ing class, has a right to make mistakes, and to profit from from them. The only thing we can learn from a mistake made by an official is that this official is liable to make mistakes, and we never will know where to find an official who can't make mistakes. As for the haphazard vote on the referendums, that is wholly due to the fact that the rank and file has no chance to know what the referendums are about; the only ones who have a fair chance to know what the referendums are good for are the delegates who discussed these referendums in the convention. We never can bave intelligent voting until we have a general medium of discussion. The referendums in the I. W. W. will remain something of a joke until we have an official bulletin in which they can be discussed. Yours for Industrial FREEDOM, B. E. NILSSON. SHALL THE LUMBER TRUST MURDER HIM? ARTHUR L. A few hours spent in the congental company of the earth's best gives one renewed energy for the fight. At 12 o'clock the festival closed, with the singing of revolutionary songs, followed by three cheers and a tiger for the day when we shall seize the reins of power and make it possible for the workers to know "the joy of living." THE FUTURE OF CHEMISTRY The meeting of the Eighth International Chemists' Congress, now in session in Washington, affords an interesting opportunity to consider what applied chemistry has done for humanity and to speculate what the future will bring forth. The most impressive fact concerning chemical knowledge is the same that characterizes most scientific knowledge-the discovery of race-benefiting processes and methods i far ahead of their adoption by the people of even the most civilized nations. If the farmer, for instance, could adopt into his work all of the chemical knowledge of soils and crops that has been gathered for him, the high cost of living might become a forgot ten issue. Farm life then would become to attractive, that the drift of humanity would be far away from the city instead of toward it. The mere matter of intelligent fertilization would result in doubling the American yield of farm crops of every description. Comparing the results ob tained on the average American farm with those secured on the progressive farmer's fields, the average American farmer wastes far more than half his opportunities, simply by his failure to mix chemistry and allied sciences with the brawn be uses. But the farmer is not the only man who wastes more than half of his energies. The coal operator mines coal by expensive processes, lifts it hundreds of feet to the surface, loads it on cars, and hauls it away, so that the value of the coal when the consumer gets it is a dozen times or more greater than its value before loosened by the miner's pick. A dozen ways have been proposed to change all of this. One of the recent propositions is to grind the coal into minute particles in the mine, mix it with water, and then pump out as they pump mud out of the underwater sections of the Panama Canal with suction dredges; ig pipe lines would then be laid from the mine to the contiguous cities, and the liquid coal carried as petroleuin is carried now. Another suggestion for the utilization of coal without mining it and transporting it by rail comes from Sir William Ramsay, the noted English chemist, who now is attending the Chemists' Congress in this country. He says that it is feasible to bore holes down to the coal stratum and through them fire the coal by electricity. Air would be sent down only in sufficient quantities to provide a regulated fire, which would transform the coal directly into producer gas. Great gas tanks would be located at the mouth of the well, and they would be connected with gas engines, which, in turn, would drive great dynamos for the making of electric cur- He estimates that this current could be delivered several hundred miles with much less loss of energy than characterizes the present wasteful methods of coal mining. rent. The savings that would thus be effected in the delivery of power from the unmined coal to the driving shafts of big factories, find a counterpart in those affected already in metal mining operations through smelters and the like. With these millions of tons of ores are secured that would otherwise be unavailable because the amount of metal in a given quantity of ore would not be sufficient to justify separation under old method. In these days of brass and copper and steel, prices would be prohibitive but for the art of chemical and electro-chemical extraction devised by the modern chemical engineer. The modern chemist is going to the ant and considering his ways in many fields. Every one realizes that our lighting bills are too heavy, and that we use vastly more energy than we should in generating our illuminants. If we could make our light with as little expenditure of energy as does the common fire-fly a gas. bill that now is $5 would be 15 cents. In a large number of chemical laboratories scientists are trying to master the mysteries of the fire-fly's lighting establishment. While there is not mach hope that they will be able to tell us at any early date how to reduce our gas bills to so great an extent, they do hold out hope that we will soon get our illumination at infinitely less expenditure of energy than we now get it. Only recently a French chemist anounced that he has succeeded in produea cold electric light, which gives off at with the expenditure of the twodredth part of the electrical current quired for producing light with the ordi nary filament lamp. This would be even more economical than the fire-fly, but whether it can be used outside of the laboratory or not remains to be seen. The inventor claims that with this light a boy or girl could carry around anywhere a battery powerful enough to light an ordinary dwelling. While the matter of talking by wire has been in use so long that people consider it nothing unusual now, sending pictures by wire still excites great interest. The newspapers of Paris now are doing that right along, however, thanks to the genius of the chemist who has made it possible to transmit half-tones. The doings at Monte Carlo are being illustrated in this way, although the intervening distance is some 500 miles. A copper film is prepared by photographic means, so as to give it the little dots of a half-tone. Gelatine, remains on the film where the shadows appear and the copper is bare where the lights are encountered. An electric needle passes over the plate, making a contact and sending electric impulses which correspond to the lights and shadows of the picture. At the receiving end there is a similar copper film untreated. A ray of light shines through a pin hole, and a shut ter cuts it off, and makes it weak or strong, according to the contact of the needle at the sending end. When the whole film has thus received its impressions of light and shadow it is developed and turned over to the photo-engraving room for reproduction. Will we ever be able to see by wire? This is a question often asked, and usually answered in the negative by the layman. But he who has seen the rise of the telegraph, the telephone and the wireless apparatus and bas considered how all of these were regarded as impossible in their turn, will scarcely deny the possibility of such an invention. All of these things spring indirectly from the chemical wizard, and he is not ready to admit that there is no possibility of further progress. Not only have pictures been transmitted by wire but by wireless, though, of course, with less success. If once we succeed in transmitting pictures by wireless as well as they are transmitted by wire, will it not be logical to suppose that seeing by wire will be the next step? Think of the infinite possibilities of a telephone system that will be able to let the persons at either end of the wire see the other! And then, if we learn how to see by wire, is it not logical to suppose that we will come to see by wireless? Didn't we first use the telegraph and then the telephone, and didn't the wireless telegraph succeed the telephone and the wireless telephone follow the wireless telegraph? And haven't they all been succeeded by the wire transmission of images, and it in turn by their wireless transmission? One might write a page instead of column upon the possibilities of chemistry and chemical engineering in the future. So many discoveries are now ready to pass out of the laboratory stage into the prac tical application stage that merely to mention some of them shows what possibilities lie in the future. For instance, a German sciențist has discovered a chemical substitute for the X-ray By mixing two special liquids and applying them to the subject to be examined, whether it be an animal, a vegetable, wood, bone, or what not, he is able to render it transpar ent. For instance, a human shoulder blade may be rendered so transparent that writing may be seen through it as through a window glass. Another chemist is experimenting with the white corpuscles of the blood. It has been conclusively proved that they are the scavengers as well as the defenders of the human body. With a high-powered microscope one may see them swallowing and digesting live germs. Old age comes from their loss of activity and pow er, and germ-borne diseases get a foothold only when they are asleep at the switch. It is hoped that there may yet be discovered a chemical compound which will stimulate them when they grow weak and keep them at work when they grow tired. This would prove a boon of inestimable value to humanity, and it is a quest that is not without hope. The secret of the chemical changes that produce life is being diligently sought in many laboratories. Artificially fertilized sea-urchin eggs have hatched, and other successes. tell that the biochemists are nearing the day when the greatest of all human mysteries will be laid bare. By Frederic J. The railroads in the Atlantic seaboard states are having great difficulty in obtaining labor for ordinary maintenance of way The scarcity of men is attributed to the extensive road building by state and county governments. work We have managed to keep our full force in the transportation department, without much trouble," said an official of the Lehigh Valley Railroad, "but it is harder to get men for work on the tracks than for many years past. We are told everywhere that the building of public roads is what takes the men away from railroad jobs. When the road building stops in cold weather we'll be able to get all the men we want but we won't need. them then much more than the road builders do. The railroads have to do most of their re-alignment, ballasting and laying of new rails and ties in the warm months of the year. "The shortage of labor is not because of any extraordinary demand created by extensions and improvements. It is difficult to keep even the regular section gangs up to their normal strength. Of course, the shortage is felt all the more keenly when improvements are under way, and it has been a serious handicap in our third and fourth tracking this summer." W. strike at the Appleton Counter factory on Railroad avenue, Bradford district, that may result in court ac Fifty scabs were brought on here tion. from New York yesterday by an employment agent named C. W. Scott, and according to the Haverhill Gazette, were given their fare back to that city this morning by the firm. Some of those men came into I. W. w. beadquarters todev and told a horrible story of how they were tricked to come here from New York. The strike breakers were hired in a cheap lodging house i New York City and the men were certain that the lodging house keeper got a nice "divvy" out of it and didn't care a rap what became of the poor breakers after they got out of New York. They were promised "all traveling expenses, $8 per week for two weeks, with board and lodging, while learning." Then if they were able to do the work and liked it they were to be put on by the piece when they could make up to $20 per week." But if not adapted for that kind of work, they would be "sent back to New York with all expenses paid, etc." On arriving at the factory here, the strike-breakers found that they had been tricked into signing a contract without looking at the back of it, which read that they were to receive $8 per week and pay $3 for board and lodging and that 10 per cent would be taken out for transportation until that expense had been cleared up." They refused to go to work on these terms, saying they were given to understand that they were to get $8 per week and lodging with the transportation thrown in. W Any wage worker, wishing to become a member of the Industrial Workers of the World, may proceed in the following manner: 1. If you live in a locality where there is a union of your industry or a mixed (recruiting) union already in existence, apply to the secretary of that local union. will furnish you with an application blank containing the Preamble to the 1. W W. Constitution and the two questions which each candidate for admission must answer in the affirmative. The questions are as follows: "Do you agree to abide by the constitution and regulations of this organization?" "Will you diligently study its principles and make vourself acquainted with its purposes?" The initiation fee is fixed by the Local Union, but cannot be more than $5.00 in any instance, and is usually $1.00 or less. The month! dues cannot exceed $1.00 and are in most locals from 35 to 50 cents 2. If there is no Local Union of the I. W. W. in your vicinity, you may be come a Member-at-Large by making application to the General Secretary, whose address is given below. You will be required to answer affirmatively the two above questions, and pay an initiation fee of $2.00. The monthly dues are $1.00 for Members-at-Large. 3. Better still, write to the General Secretary for a Charter Application Blank. Get no less than TWENTY signatures thereon, of bonafide wage workers in any one industry (for a Local Industrial Union) or in several industries (for a Local Recruiting or mixed Union) and send the charter application with the names to the General Secretary,, with the $10.00 charter fee.
