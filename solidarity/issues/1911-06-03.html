O., Calif, May 21. The redwood lumber district of Northern California comprises the counties of Del Norte, Humboldt and Mendocino. The largest towns are Crescent City, Eureka and Fort Bragg. There are about 25,000 lumber workers working in this district. If organized in one body with some knowledge of revolutionary tacties they could win a strike for shorter hours, more wages and better conditions, because they WOULD CONTROL THE RED WOOD MARKET OF THE WORLD. The lumber is shipped to San Francisco, Manila, Honolulu and Australia I am told that there is part of Australia where no other can be used because the ants would eat it, and they will not touch red wood. Some of the redwood trees measure from 16 to 38 feet in diameter. Most of the timber land has been stolen, and is owned by rich and powerful lumber barons. Discontent Among Workers. There is very much discontent among the workers, and the wages are getting lower every year. At one time the work. ers hete were steady men with homes and families, but now the company boats bring men every day from the employment offices of San Francisco, the home of craft unige. TWO PROMISING MOVEMENTS Two movements of great promise for the immediate and ultimate, unfolding of the revolutionary struggle in America are gaining ground rapidly at the present tim. These are the proposals of a universal eight hour workday in 1919, and a general strike of the working class on the opening day of the MeNaniars, trial in Los Angeles. Both originated with the 1. W. W. and from all sections of the country comes evidence that these proposals are touching the workers in a vital spot, and are there fore meeting with, ready response. The cap Miners The general depression in industry with an enormously enlarged army of unem ployed, also offers the right soil for the rapid growth of the eight hour movement. Even capitalists admit that industrial panies cannot be avoided by them. Witness, for example the statement of C. A. Coma at the recent meeting of the National Association of Manufacturers held in New York, who said: "There will always be industrial panies, which result from overproduction production done rapidly than the people are able to consume." more But the workers don't want always to be the victims of these industrial panics, which our employers admit they cannot prevent. So the workers are beginning to ask, "How can we put a stop to these periodie depressions in industry, which bring untold suffering upon ourselves and families?" And of course the only answer is, "To oust the employing class froin the control and direction of industry." That can only be done by organizing the collective labor power of the working class at the point of production, for the purpose of supplanting capitalism. Hence the readiness with which the working class is taking to the eight-hour movement. The power to minimize the effects of industrial panies, and to finally abolish them altogether, is in the hands of the working class, and NOT the master class. IN THE SOUTH Reasons Why the Industrial Union is a Good Thing for Arkansas as Well as the Rest of the World. (From the Huntington, Ark., Herald) In another column of the Advocate will be found a news item of the organization of the lumber workers by the Industrial Workers of the World. This organization is organized along the lines that are not in accord with the tenets of true unionism, and these "wonder workers" have caused unionism much trouble, as they create a prejudice against organized labor that is bard to ovecome. It is to be hoped that they will not gain a foot-hold in Arkansas: The Union Advocate. We are fearful that the editor of the Union Advocate does not understand the Industrial Workers of the World. It is not a bad organization, indeed it is a good labor organization, one that we believe, must finally dominate the labor movement, not only of America, but of the world. It depends altogether upon what we mean by the term "true unionism" when we speak. The Industrial Workers of the World propose that the workers shall be organized by industries instead of by crafts. For instance in the lumber industry, the A. F. of L. would organize the loggers, the sawyers, the yard men and engineers and kiln men in different crafts, as many erafts as possibie, with all of them working under different contracts expiring at different times, so that when there arose a dispute between one craft and the employer and a strike of that craft or branch of the industry was called the other crafts would keep on working or scabbing on the craft or division on strike: The Industrial Workers of the World would organize all the workers in one industry so that an injury to any worker in any department would be considered an injury to any other worker in any other department. They would have no contract with the employer, but would be ready, at a moment's notice, to strike to enforce their just demands. But the best feature of the Industrial labor organization is yet to be stated. It is a class conscious organization. It declares that the employes and the employers of labor have no interests in common; that there is an eternal conflict between them, between those who buy labor power and those who sell labor power. They teach that since the employer wants to buy labor power he wants to buy it at the lowest possible price, and that the worker having only his labor power to sell wants to sell it at the highest possible price; that. the employer wants his workmen to work the longest hours, while the workmen want to work the shortest hours; that this conflict of interest is manifest in every struggle and can no longer be hidden by the false cry that the interests of the laborer and the capitalist are identical. PREAMBLE The working elass and the employing class have nothine in common. There can be no peace so long as hunger and want are found among millions of working people and the few, who make up the employing class, have all th good things of life. Between these two classes a struggle must go on until the workers of the world organize as a class, take possession of the earth and the machinery of production, and abolish the wage system. We find that the centering of the management of industries into fewer and fewer hands makes the trades unions unable to cope with the ever-growing power of the employing class. The trade unions foster a state of affairs which allows one set of workers to be pitted against another set of workers in the same industry, thereby helping defeat one another in wage wars. Moreover, the trade unions aid the employing class to mislead the workers into the belief that the working class have interests in common with their employers. These, conditions can be changed and the interest of the working class upheld only by an organization formed in such a way that all its members in any one industry, or in all industries if necessary, cease work whenever a strike or lockout is on in any department thereof, thus making an injury to one an injury to all. Instead of the conservative motto, "A fair day's wages for a fair day's work," we must inscribe on our banner the revolutionary watchword, "Abolition of the wage system." It is the historie mission of the working class to do away with capitali The army of production must be organized , not only for the every-day struggle with capitalists; but also to carry on production when capitalism shall have been overthrown. By organizing industrially we are forming the structure of the new society within the shell of the old. Knowing, therefore, that such an organization is absolutely necessary for our.emancipation we unite under the following constitution. International Conference of Painters May 7, at Munich, Bavaria, an interns tional conference of painters was opened, with delegates present from Germany, Austria, Hungary , Denmark, Switzerland, Holland and Sweden. Germany was represented by 14 delegates, and the other countries each by one delegate. The president (Tobler of Germany) in his opening speech explained the necessity of constant relations between the organizations of painters and decorators of all countries, a necessity which is becoming all the more urgent as the employers have already organized themselves very strongly and have taken action internationally on several important questions. Therefore it is necessary for the workers to enter into relations with the organizations of England, France, Belgium and the United States in order to create an International Secretariat of Paint- In the discussions the delegates showed themselves all in favor of international relations, also with Italy, as one of the delegates remarked. It was decided to hold the first international congress of painters in Switzerland, if possible in September, or otherwise the spring of 1912. Lockout Possible in Denmark. (Adopted by the Detroit Mich., Eight Hour Conference, Sunday, May 14 1911.) CALIFORNIA REDWOODS (Continued From Page One) How could we win the strike? First, organize a general, strike committee, with alternates in case of arrest. Call out all the men, establish camps, also means of communication, and print a strike bulletin. Keep up enthusiasm by having speakers address the strikers every day, distribute literature and song books, and teach them to sing revolutionary songs. Print 10,000 calls for funds and send them to every labor organization. Picket the employment offices. In case of injunctions, fill up the jails. If the companies can get plenty of men as strikebreakers, then stop picketing, send L. W. W. men on the job, work a while and call a second strike. And don't stick your fingers in the cog wheels; put something else in their places. And don't chop the big trees down; pull them up by the roots with the line. Give the strike publicity through the press and big mass meetings. Remember, it would be hard to get 25,- 000, scabs, and if the militia was called out they would have to patrol hundreds of miles and guard every mill and cainp. If we were losing ground we could resumé production again for a month; by that time we could get more funds and food, and the militia would be disbanded. Last but not least, we could use sabotage if we were forced back; it could be used to perfection in the lumber industry. We could maintain an inforinotion bureau, and get employment for blacklisted members elsewhere, or we could put an assessment on the railways. Carmen are working very long hours and if their hours were reduced to 8 a day, another 20,000 carmen would be required. As evidence that some progress is being made may mention the port of Bristol; one year ago out of 6,000 port workers only 1,000 were organized; at the present moment 5,000 are organized and are ready for internatienal action. Unfortunately, at Southhampton and Hull organization is very backward and we are now at work to alter this: As showing how machinery for loading and unloading has affected the dockers, I may instance the small boats carrying about 130 tons trading between Southhampton and Cherbourg, Honfleur, St. Malo and other French ports. A few years ago each of these boats found employment for 80 dockers for 12 hours, now as the result of the hydraulic gear they require only one-sixth of the number formerly drawn for the same cargo. There is much more cargo coming to Southhampton than ever before but the working conditions of the dockers are very bad.
