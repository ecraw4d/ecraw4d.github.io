Mattingly To strike is divine. Only submission is sinful and vile. It used to be said, "You can kill men; but you cannot kill ideas." Now it is coming to be realized that ideas are only valuable in so far as they have live men back of them. Rally to the defense of Tresca, Little, Gilday, Schmidt, and all the others in jail at Duluth. Be a live idealist. Make your defense offensive. Show to the world why men must be defended: because they have dared to organize their lows for improvement and freedom. Make your defense an agitation for overthrow of capitalism; and the building up of the new society within the shell of the old. In Solidarity of April 22, we quoted "a summary of a report made last November by the New York State Industrial Commission. According to this report in a group of more than a thousand factories last October the number of operatives had increased seven per cent; the total of wages paid, 16 per cent and the value of the output 34 per cent, as compared with the previous year." (See editorial "Industrial Prog ress in the United States.") We then said, "With machinery and efficiency increasing out put out of proportion to the labor forces employed and the wages paid, capitalist profits must be increasingly large, SO LONG AS A MARKET CAN BE HAD." Without markets, production outruns consumptica, and a slow-down becomes inevitable, together with industrial distress and revolutionary activity. That is just what is happening. War munition markets are being closed, men are being laid off, and the beginning of the end of the Price war psychological prosperity is already in sight. As Mr. Banker says of the situation he describes presages some serious difficulties when it becomes necessary to bring the pay-level back to somewhere near its pre-war mark." Why need it go back there? Why not give to labor all that it produces, just eliminating capitalist profit, and enabling consumption and production to be as one-in equilibrium all the time? Why "reactions," if not for capitalist profits? One word more, and we'll finish: What will be the effect of this "moderate financial and commercial reaction" on the prospects of Mr. Wilson's re-election? And in how far will this "reaction" be accentuated, in order to bring about Mr. Wilson's defeat, and make the beginnings of Mr. Hughes' reign auspicious ones? It goes to show that the capitalists are willing to spend considerable money in order to be in a position to influence "public opinion" and to create conditions favorable to their own class interests. For Mr. Munsey's paper is not the only high-priced newspaper in this country. There are many of them, owned as Mr. Munsey's are, on the chain system, just like a chain of stores. Another Theory Smashed What's the matter with the Price theory that war is a psychological stimulant resulting in years of unbounded prosperity? Readers of Solidarity will recall that, in the issue of March 11 of this year, the Price theory was criticised and condemned as unsound. Economic history was quoted to show that every stimulation is followed by relaxation and prostration; that every great impetus in capitalist production is followed by years of reaction and stagnation. Emphasis was especially laid on the fact that such reaction was today likely to be more rapid than in the past. Now the unexploited territories of 50 years ago Western United States, Canada, China, India, et al. are no longer awaiting development as before. That the undeveloped fields of yesterday are the competitors of today; and that, finally, such have been the achievements of invention and science that production proceeds more rapidly than consumption than formerly, and thus makes a more rapid reaction certain. That was printed in Solidarity of March 11. In his weekly paper, "Commerce and Finance," of July 5, only four short months afterwards, Mr. Price already sings a different song than the one condemned on March 11. He is impressed by the apathy on the New York Stock Exchange, "where a slackening of the speculative demand for stocks and bonds generally precedes a recession in distributive trade." Accordingly he makes inquiry among "many men who are influential în shaping what may balled the mind of the Stock Exchange." What does he find? Just this: "We find that a majority of them are inclined to expect a moderate financial and commercial reaction." One is almost tempted to exclaim, What! so soon? Where are the years of, unbounded prosperity promised by the Price psychological theory of war? Where, oh, where, are they? Mr. Munsey.
