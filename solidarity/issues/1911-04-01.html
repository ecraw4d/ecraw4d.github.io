Pensions Only For Legalized Butchers. Pensions are not granted to carriers, as they are engaged in a necessary and peace ful occupation, pensions being allowed only to legalized butchers, and those who have held "high" positions, the supposition being that the carrier and all others get ting a meager wage should save something for their old age and sickness. We were formerly allowed fifteen consecutive days for vacation, but they finally came to the conclusion that we were eti titled to 15 working days, and here is where the "economy" plan shines most of Formerly a substitufe was assigned to work upon a route for the entire period of a carrier's vacation, but now, they will only allow two, three or four hours subhire each day; and in some places none at all, which makes it necessary to omit some deliveries and collections, and also compells the two remaining carriers to perform the work which was done by three men. Carriers work in crews of three. Substitutes are paid at the rate of thirty cents per hour for this service, and when you take into consideration that they were formerly paid the munificent sum of $1.67 per day, and now only 2, 3, or 4 hours at the rate of 30cts, you can readily see what they can save by this scheme. New York was yesterday the scene of one of the most horrible sacrifices of working class life to capitalist profit that ever afficted the metropolis and perhaps the nation. One hundred and thirty-four working, girls and men were killed and ded in the fire that gutted the tenry "fireproof building of The Triangle shirtwaist Co., at Washington place and Greene st. Trapped in lofts, with doors locked, to keep them at work, and with no fire escapes down which they could descend to safety, the panic-stricken workers, who were mostly girls, leaped from the windows in such numbers that it actu ally seemed to the firemen below to rain human beings. Down they came, in twos and threes, burying the firemen and those who assisted them under the terrible down pour. Many were also fearfully burned. The morgue, to which the victims wêre taken, resembled a battle field in its hor rible display of corpses. The bodies were charred, smashed, ripped open, headless, without arms and legs, roasted and distorted by all the agonies of sudden and violent death. It was a sight such as Dante must have beheld in the Inferno and such as only a Dore could imagine. Of course, no one is definitely respon sible for this most appalling and neelfles sacrifice of workers' lives. The coroner says so; the fire chief says so; in fact, all the capitalist agencies say so. It will not do to jeopardize the capitalist method of obtaining profit by murdering the working class. That was shown in the recent disastrous fire at Newark, N..J., where the workers' lives were freely given and the capitalists who profited from their labor as freely permitted to remain at large. R. Hearst and his Chicago pa pers against the International Typographical Union, and the lame tactics displayed by the latter toward that "friend of labor." It was shown how the Publishers' Association displayed the class consciousness of the bosses, while craft division and the alasurd idea of "identical interests between slaves and masters" rendered the printers' union like a foot ball in the hands of their exploiters. Additional information is here given with reference to another of the allied printing trades organizations the Intertional Pressmen and Assistants' Union of North America. Time and again have the printers contended that their unions were unassailable and by reason of "strong organisation and conservative dealing with employers through contracts" they had been enabled to secure for themselves better wage and hour conditions, than almost any other crafts. And while industrial unionists have conceded that their peculiar situation in our industrial system has afforded the printing trades advantages not so easily gained by other crafts, at the same time. we have contended that due to craft division and mutual scabbery through contracts, the printers have gained little compared with what they should and would get by united action. The dominant craft in the allied printing trades is the International Typographical Union. It controls 50 per cent of the vote in Printing Trades Councils, and thereby practically dominates those bodies in its own interést. For all practical purposes, each craft fights its own battles with the bosses alone, the others remaining at work under contract, thereby enabling the bosses association to easily compromise or whip each union in turn. On the other hand, as was shown in the Hearst fight, the Publishers' Association acts as a unit, on the principle that "an injury to one publisher is an injury to all." Plea to the Interesting and important developsients are taking place throughout this country; developments that should cause every man and woman to give thought to their deep significance and far reaching consequence. As Solidarity has repeatedly of late pointed out, there is now being waged a bitter warfare against every form of unionism, in the capitalist determination to reduce, wages and subjugate the American working class to its profit-making requirements. But it seems, to judge from recent events, the capitalists are not going to stop at unionism alone; they are going to wage a virulent attack upon every symptom or prospective form of revolt that manifests itself, no matter how harmless or how in significant it may appear to be. Capital ism is determined to stamp out every vestige of challenge against its undisputed control and necessity to rule. More than that, the conservative attitude of the printers' unions and their contract system enables the publishers to deal directly with pliable or corruptible "leaders," whose actions show them to be in many cases the dupes or willing tools of the bosses. Socialists for the working class to be strong it must protect itsslf economically; it must contest the supremacy of the capitalist class in the shop, the mill, the mine, etc. It must first be strong economically; all else follows. Once the working class develops economic power it develops the power to dominate society. If this is not true, then socialism is a falsehood and the Socialist Party is without foundation in fact. Now, to attain this power the working class must organize as a class into one, big union. It must face the concentration of capital with the concentration of labor. This it is prevented from doing by the old form of trades unionism, that is supported by the Civic Federation, and the leaders of which betray the working class on every occasion, as witness the Bethiehem steel strike, the Yonkers elevator strike, the Chicago garment workers strtke and last, but not least of all, the New York express drivers strike, one of the most barefaced betrayals of labor in all the history of the American labor movement. According to the national bulletin of the Socialist Party, the executive committee of the Socialist Party of Nevada complains that the active members of the latter are being discharged by the Southern Pacific Railroad. At Toole Utah, and Anaconda, Mont., all the socialists employed by The International Smelting and Refining Co., which has works at both places, were dis charged. A couple of years ago, prior to the big strike there, 45 socialists were discharged from the employment of the Beth lehem Steel Co. There are, most likely, other cases of similar importance occuring in all the large corporations every day. This state of affairs should cause Socialist Party members everywhere to pause and give certain fundamental facts and principles deep consideration. It is evident that the ballot is fiot a job-protecter. It is also evident that by means of job manipulation, the ballot can be seriously af fected. The capitalists are determined to cripple working class action in the bud, the point of production; that is, in shop, mine, mill, etc. With the radicals, rebels and revolutionists starved into sub mission, as many of them will be, the working class, will be economically weak. Capitalism will win out; for the class that is weak economically is weak in beery other respect. All power resides in economic strength in the strength that controls the means by which all classes live. It of course follows from the above, that The Socialist Party is not in favor of one big class union. It favors neutrality in matters relating to trades unions, as if there could be neutrality where labor is being betrayed in labor's name; and where its own members are being victimized and their ballot undermined. This policy binds socialists to their own destruction. It compels them to refrain from aggressive work in behalf of revolutionary industrial unionisin. It further, binds then to the perpetuation of Gompers and the Civie Federation, and through them, of the capitalist class and capitalism. As a socialist and a wage worker, the writer urges all Socialist Party members to unite with their fellow workers in the 1. W. W., in the aggressive upbuilding of revolutionary industrial unionism, in the knowledge that once that is accomplished there will be real and not impotent political action, as at present. Industrial unity first, last and all the time, that alone will secure working class triumph in all its manifestations. Workers of the world unite in the industries of the world! this is the modern setting of an old rallying ery, demanded by modern conditions. New York, March 23. An S. P. Voter.

Fifth Ave. Agitate for the 8-hour day! SOLIDARITY "THE RIGHTS OF MAN" A TALE OF THE FORUM By The Commentator. It was a merry and lusty trio of workingmen that came bustling out of Hart ley's Hall. They were convulsed with laughter and scarcely able to keep their feet, such was the difficulty they experienced in wrestling with their merriment. Hartley's Hall is located in every industrial city. Its noted Sunday afternoon forum" is often the scene of amusing happenings. Social theories of variegated hues and textures are there propounded and expounded, Then they are either exposed or exalted, pulverized or praised, maligded or magnified, condemned or commended, as the case might be, in open discussion. "It all depends on the viewpoint," as the chairman would say. And the viewpoint was as numerous as fleas on a dog, and often as pernicious; hence the fun that would frequently ensue. Tom McAllister was coming along the avenue when, near Hartley's Hall, he was unceremoniously jostled by the trio described in the opening of this tale. Tom resented this ungentlemanly conduct. He was engaged in deep and painful thought at the time. He was figuring out the problem of how to become a billionaire like Rockefeller on the debts arising from $15 a week's wages, an unsteady job, a family, and prices and rents soaring, areoplane-like, ever higher. Tom thought he was just getting a glimpse of the solution, when the bunch collided with him and nearly knocked him off his feet He bristled up-and was about to retaliate, when a familiar voice called out from among the trio: "Hello, Tom, where were you? You missed it." To which were added in quick succession the following greetings and comments: "Hello there, Tom. You ought to have been around; we had more fun than you could shake a stick at. "Just like you, Tom. You're damned serious and taken up with problems that you are never around when there is something funny doing!" "Oh," said Tom, "cut that out. What's it all about, anyway?" A renewed peal of laughter was the only answer he received. "Say, Joe, what's got into you fellows anyway? What's the joke? Let me into it, will you?" This appes had an effect. "It's this way," said Joe. "There was one of those sky pilots in there this afternoon, who thought he knew something about the labor question. But I guess he learned that the fellow, who but tons his collar in the back doesn't know it all; in fact, he has a good deal coming to him by way of the education route. Then Joe paused to laugh as he recalled the affair; while the others joined him in a hearty manner. "And the beauty of it was, chimed in John Sutor, some of his congregation were there to see what a fool he could be. "Begin at the beginning." interposed Sandy Thompson, "or else Tom will think the joke is on us more than on the other fellow. "Right you are," cried Tom. "Well," responded, Joe, "to begin with, his name is the Right, Rev. Father Doorbell; his specialty, the place of religion in the settlement of social problems; his particular hobby, war on the abuses of unionism and the assertion of the right of the individual and the aberty of man to work when and where he pleases." "Ah," said Tom, relaxing into a broad smile. "I see a light. "A man," continued Joe, "of a husky and corpulent build that reflected his syiritual nature; dogmatic speech intended to be convincing and admitting of no dis pute, and a subtle manner of palming off presumptive evidence for indisputable proof." "Do you recognize the species, Tom?" asked Sandy. "I do," answered Tom, smiling more broadly and seeing a brighter light than before. "Well," resumed Joe, "he came, saw, and suddenly had an important engagement, which broke up the meeting in a tumult all because some workingmen challenged his assertions and submitted his argument to the test of facts." 'Ah, the deuce, lamented Sandy. "Why run ahead of the story, any way?" "Oh," said John, "you know Joe al ways claims we waste too much time on the clergy. Whereat all hands laughed again. "Well," said Joe, straightening out bis face, Father Doorbell said the unions were guilty of violence and murder. They restricted individual rights. They should be fair, manly, law-abiding and decent; keeping open the way to opportunity, instead of limiting the rights of the individ ual and infringing on the liberties of man. The unions blew up the Times in Los Angeles. They killed and frightened off men willing to work for wages which they themselves did not want. He favored unions, but opposed their abuses, which he believed were due to the loud-mouthed and the vicious, who secured control he cause they were more brazen than the others. He favored a minimum wage law below which employers could not go when paying men. And you fellows found a cause for hil arity in all that?" asked, Tom, with mock seriousness. 'Ah," said Joe, "the fun began when workingman got up and asked the Rev. Father this series of questions: "Is it not a fact that the jurisprudence and established sense of fair play of this country, require that a man or body of men accused of a crime be considered innocent until proven guilty beyond the shadow of a doubt? Is it not a fact that in the Times explosion no trial has been held, no evidence properly adduced to prove that the unions are responsible for the explosion? Is it not then an act of injustice, a lack of decency and self-respect on your port, and an insult to our intelligence, to accuse the unions of blowing up the Times, as you do?" "Of course," said Joe parenthetically, everyone enjoyed the blunt questioning of the workingmen, with its complete lack of respect for ecolesiastical authority and its determination to render homage only to the truth." PREAMBLE The working class and the employing class have nothing in common. There can be no peace so long as hunger and want are found among millions of working people and the few, who make up the employing class, have all the good things of life. Between these two classes a struggle must go on until the workers of the world organize as a class, take possession of the earth and the machinery of production, and abolish the wage system. We find that the centering of the management of industries into fewe and fewer hands makes the trades unions unable to cope with the ever-growing power of the employing class. The trade unions foster a state of affairs which allows one set of workers to be pitted against another set of workers in the same industry, thereby helping defeat one another'in wage wars. Morcover, the trade unions aid the employing class to mislead the workers into the belief that the working class have interests in common with their employers. These conditions can be changed and the interest of the working class upheld only by an organization formed in such a way that all its members in any one industry, or in all industries if necessary, cease work whenever a strike or lockout is on in any department thereof, thus making an injury to one an injury to all. Instead of the conservative motto, "A fair day's wages for a fair day's work," we must inscribe on our banner the revolutionary watchword, "Abolition, of the wage system." It is the historic mission of the working class to do away with capitalism. The army of production must be organized, not only for the every-day struggle with capitalists, but also to carry on production when capitalism shall have been overthrown. By organizing industrially we are forming the structure of the new society within the shell of the old. The sooner the railroaders throw him off of their backs the better it will be for them. He is a capitalist decoy duck. Labor Abroad. Ten thousand men employed in the Singer Sewing Machine Works, near Kilbowie, Scotland, about eight miles from Glasgow, are on strike on account of a dispute over wages. Rioting is reported in the Welsh could fields, where strike has been in progress several weeks. Three thousand miners attacked the big colliery of the Clydach Vale Company and burned the sheds and mine buildings. Two hundred and fifty police, who were rushed to the scene, charged the rioters with drawn batons and finally drove them away. It is expected there will be serious trouble, and the mili tia has been ordered held in readiness for service. Case of drive, drive, drive. Carriers' Association no Good. The association of letter carriers, which has a membership of something over 20,000 members, stands idly by and permits all these drastic inflictions without a murmur, when by a united protest they could very easily make the high moguls understand that they were not the whole thing. Too long have they looked upon them selves as belonging to the aristocracy of labor; in fact, they did not look upon themselves as belonging to the working class at all. To mention Industrialism or the Labor movement to these would be cock. roach capitalists is like shaking a red rag at a bull. When Congress passed the bill increasing our wages it stated that carriers, after five years of service, should receive compensation at the rate of $1,200 per annum. But the department makes a fine distinction here, as it only appoints 50 per cent of these who are eligible holding the others over until some future date. In their greed for profits, they are now facing suits for overtime, for according to a recent decision they should not work the carrier force more than 48 hours per week, but entirely overlooking that, they compelled one carrier to report for work on Sunday, each carrier taking his turn. But now orders have been issued to cut out all Sunday work, except that which is absolute ly necessary, those compelled to work on Sunday being granted time off during the week, equivalant to the time put in on Sunday. This time off applies only to collectors, the carriers on routes being ordered to report earlier on Monday, and under no consideration whatsoever, must they leave the office after schedule time. This making it necessary for those having heavy routes to bunch their mail and set it up in the, hallway of some building, or other convenient place, and strict orders are issued that we must also return on schedule time. Talk about hustling, whew! Carriers Waking Up. Experience is a good teacher, for it gives the writer great pleasure to state that the carriers are at last beginning to realize that they are only ordinary working men, and that they must align themselves with their brothers in other lines of industry. Shake off that laissez faire feeling and turn your organization into something, more than a coffin club, for that is all that it is at present. Big Industrial Union Needed. Every day sees more and more of them enlightened, and as their hopes of better things in any other way are doomed to disappointment, they must-see the neces sity to organize along with the office clerks, R. R. clerks, telegraphers, telephone oger ators, and all others connected with the intelligence department into one grand big union, and, with the help of their brothers in other industries, wrest the control of the means of production and distribution and exchange away from those who have stolen them and give them back to their rightful owners. Now, carriers, you are dissatisfied with your lot. You feel that you are being unjustly dealt with by your superiors! You know or should know that it is the labor of yourselves and other workers who produce all the wealth of the world, and you should know that by reorganizing into a union stated above, you can overturn this whole damnable profit taking and soul killing system, and bring about such a system that you will get the full product of your toil, own and manage the industries yourselves, elect your own superin tendent and chief clerks, who, if they do not carry out your wishes can be easily recalled.
