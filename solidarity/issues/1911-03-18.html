Activity in Lawrence (Special to Solidarity) Lawrence, Mass., March 10. In a former article, I wrote that Local 20, I. W. W., of Lawrence had joined the Alliance of Textile Workers' Unions of Lawrence. Our local, having voted at its last meet ing to take an active part in the agitation for a universal eight hour workday, as proposed by the Western 1. W. W. locals, our delegates to the Alliance brought the matter before that body. The principle was endorsed; but on account of the small number of delegates present, discussion of the eight hour question was postponed until the meeting of March 12. Following our proposition, it was voted to start agitation with a yiew to organize all workers of the textile industry. A mass meeting will be held shortly, with speakers of different-languages and of dif ferent organizations. Here is the opportunity, for the Indus trial Workers to make themselves known. The capitalist press hitherto has maintained a conspiracy of silence concerning our existence, notwithstanding we have had several organizations in Lawrence, have held numerous street meetings, distributed much literature and had several strikes. While the papers have preserved silence regarding the 1. W. W., they have record ed every move of the independent or craft unions. But now, by the proposed, agitation on a larger scale, we will assuredly put an. end to this conspiracy of silence. We propose to cover the city with I. W. W. literature. Pending the projected mass meeting, we have planned to distribute thousands of leaflets, preambles and several hundred copies of Solidarity.. In preparation for this agitation and that of the eight hour movement, Local 20 has elected a Propaganda Committee, which has already outlined a plan of campaign which we hope will be attended with success. The time is favorable for the spread of our principles and methods. A partial strike in the Ayer Mills, a branch of the American Woolen Co., and which ended in defeat, thanks to craft union methods, will add impulse to our activity. Very recently, an Italian branch of Local 20 was organized, and in this connection, we are planning to bring Organizer Joseph J. Ettor to Lawrence, if possible, to aid the Italian branch in organizing all workers of that nationality. At the same time, he will be useful for the general agitation. In spite of the great efforts made, we have failed hitherto to interest the English speaking workers in industrial unionism. But we hope by this projected agitation we may overcome their indifference, and that, aided by all locals of the 1. W. W., as well as by other unions, we may continue the agitation until we have conquered the eight hour day as a preliminary step toward other conquests, leading finally to complete freedom from the modern system of wage slavery. All to work for the CONQUEST OF MEXICO An all absorbing topic for discussion and speculation the past week has been the mobilization of one-third of the United States army and navy on the land and sea surrounding Mexico. "American intervention in the Mexican revolution," has not only been hinted ap, but boldly announced from all directions. Now even President Taft and the secretary of war admit the same as a possibility. The "conquest of Mexico" and all the territory south of there to the Panama Canal, is by some regarded as a probability. To those who view such movements as relating to the affairs of political rulers alone, this act of President Taft's is looked upon as designed simply to support Mexico's perpetual president upon his tottering throne. But such a superficial view does not sum up the situation. As a matter of fact, Mexico was conquered long since, by American and forof rulers. That "something else" can be nothing else than the organized and determined effort of the Mexican working class to overthrow the system of peonage itself.. That would indeed be a revolution, striking at the very foundations of society, and threatening the economic power of the masters at its base. Such. indeed, appears to be the case; the SOCIAL REVOLU- TION looms up as a menace to the capitalists' interests. Their property, which they have wrung from the lives' blood of the Mexican peons, may be taken back for collective use by these slaves themselves. Horror of horrors! What a possibility, and what an exampie for the rest of the world, should the attempt be made! Yet the attempt is being made, if we are to judge by reports coming from the north of Mexico, whence alone news of the revolutionary movement is allowed to cross the border. "We do not want masters; we want land and liberty. The ballot will not feed us," one little group of rebel fighters is reported to have said to the insurrecto general, Madero. And the latter promptly proved their point by arresting and holding them "We are not merely after Diaz; we are after capitalism," is the sinister meaning of their remark. as prisoners of war. Capitalism must be protected. So, another "tool of the interests" at once rushes to the rescue of his masters. Taft obeys the orders of his economic overlords in Wall Street, and sends the troops to the Mexican border and will send them in to Mexico if necessary, to crush the rebellion of the peons, And these mannikins in human form, to the number of 20,000 are only too willing to shoulder their rifles and shoot innocent workingmen whom these soldiers never saw before, and who are striving for the right to control their own product. What a lesson on the true nature of the political state as the handmaid of King Capital! It matters not what these millions of workers have suffered, and still suffer. No Taft will come to their rescue with his troops. But let a half dozen big bellies in Wall Street whisper the word into this "president of the whole people's" ear, and the cannonading of the Mexican slaves begins. And why shouldn't it be so? That's what the political state was created for: to serve as an instrument of the ruling economic class to oppress and hold in subjection the working class. The history of the State in all its forms-from the days of chattel slavery, through feudalism to the wage system, shows that it has never been used for anything else. And it never, will be used for anything else. As long as the State exists, there will be oppression of the slave class by the master class. But for all that, the political state is only an instrument; it is not the underlying cause of oppression; nor would the abolition of the State alone, if that were possible, remove oppression. The Mexican workers might overthrow Diaz and put a "benevolent" ruler in his place; but so long as the economic system of peonage remained intact, their misery and degradation would remain. It was decided that no money should be wasted in employing lawyers to enact the farce of expounding the meaning of such a plain statement as the First Amendment to the constitution of the United States. However, the court was used most effectively for propaganda. Trial after trial was held, and at each trial our position was presented to a crowded court room, by one or more of the group of members on trial. Incidentally, about 500 residents of Fresno (principally business men) were summoned, to serve on these juries. Not one of them was so disloyal to his class as to hang a jury. Workingmen who might have done so, were peremptorily challenged by the prosecuting attorney. Our appeals for necessary aid were made only to the working class, and we found ready response from that quarter. Perfect Discipline in Jail. A vast amount of outside work, locally necessary, was carried on by an outside committee, also under direction of the body in jail. All funds supplied were spent economically and to the best advantage. A notable result of the willingness at all times to fight oppression of the jailors, was the abandonment on their part of all needlessly harsh regulations and practices. PREAMBLE The working class and the employing class have nothing in common. There can be no peace so long as hunger and want are found among millions of working people and the few, who make up the employing class, have all the good things of life. Between these two classes a struggle must go on until the workers of the world organize as a class, take possession of the earth and the machinery of production, and abolish the wage system. We find that the centering of the management of industries into fewe and fewer hands makes the trades unions unable to cope with the ever-growing power of the employing class. The trade unions foster a state of affairs which allows one set of workers to be pitted against another set of workers in the same industry, thereby helping defeat one another in wage wars, Moreover, the trade unions aid the employing class to mislead the workers into the belief that the working class have interests in common with their employers. These conditions can be changed and the interest of the working class upheld only by an organisation formed in such a way that all its members in any one industry, or in all industries if necessary, cease work whenever a strike or lockout is on in any department thereof, thus making an injury to one an injury to all. Instead of the conservative motto, "A fair day's wages for a fair day's work," we must inscribe on our banner the revolutionary watchword, "Abolition of the wage system." It is the historic mission of the working class to do away with capitaliam. The army of production must be organized, not only for the every-day struggle with capitalists, but also to carry on production when capitalism shall have been overthrown. By organizing industrially we are forming the structure of the new society within the shell of the old. WORLD OF LABOR The Mobilization. The world of labor may not appear to be directly concerned in the reported mobilization of U. S. troops along the Mexican border. The workingman who keeps his nose so near the grindstone of his job as to exclude a glimpse of every thing else can possibly see no reason why he should get excited thereat. Neverthe less, it is a matter of importance to the American working class. If the American government intervenes in behalf of either Diaz or the American billions invested in Mexico, the revolution in the latter country will be given a set-back, and the de velopment of a distinct and progressive working class prevented until some future time. This will mean the continued enslavement of the Mexican population, and the presence at the southern extremity of the nation of a cheap and an enslaved la bor supply on which the capitalists of this country may draw in future conflicts. They play into capitalist hands, and build up those destructive agencies of capitalism, the army and navy. Thus, from more than one standpoint, is the American workingman concerned, and from more than one standpoint should be accordingly oppose the capitalist 'war game," now being played along the Mexican border.
