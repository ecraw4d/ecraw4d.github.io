EMANCIPATION NEW CASTLE, PENNSYLVANIA, SATURDAY, DECEMBER 18, 1009. FIGHTING FOR FREE SPEECH Police Keep Up Reign of Terror Against Industrial Unionists in Spokane, Washington. Horrible Sufferings of I. W. W. Prisoners Sweated and Starved in Jails of that City. The J. W. W. is making history in Spokane, Washington. A fight by our organisation to use the streets for public meetings has beep going on since Novem ber. Five hundred men are in jail on bread and water and amid sanitary and other conditions that are a disgrace to humanity. Many were brutally clubbed by the police, though offering no resistance to arrest. About twenty, including one woman, Elizabeth Gorley Flynn, were arrested on the charge of "criminal conspiracy," they being regarded as "leaders" by the authorities. The I. W. W. hall has been raided several times and all committeemen and other officials taken to jail on the same charge. "Conspiracy" trials have resulted in sentences of from four to six months in the county jail, Elizabeth Gurley Flynn was given a three months sentence. Last week's entire edition of the "Industrial Worker," the local organ of the L. W. W., was siezed by the police as it eame from the press and taken to the city hall to be destroyed. Five editors had been successively jailed on "conspiracy" charges. The city ordinance against street speak ing, which the 1. W. W. is fighting and which permits "religious" meetings thereby plainly discriminating against our organization, was, in the expressed opinion of Police Judge Mann, "unconstitutional." So those arrested for attempting to hold meetings are charged with "disorderly conduct" and sentenced to 30 days in jail and a fine of $100. That means 60 days or more in jail under conditions that beggar description. Scores of reenforcements are arriving daily in Spokane from all parts of the country to keep up the fight. The Coeur d'Alene, Idaho distriet union of the Western Federation of Miners has declared a boycott against all goods coming from Spokane while the fight is on. That action, followed by sitailar resolutions by Butte Miners' Union, and other organ izations of the surrounding territory, is. hitting hard the city's business interests. Several thousand dollars additional expense a week are required to pay extra officers, feed the prisoners, provide hospital serxices, ete. It is costing the tax payers dearly to "suppress the I. W. W. Meanwhile the fight goes on with unabated vigor on the side of our organization, Money is needed to fight the court cases and otherwise to carry on the struggle. It is a struggle for existence on the part of the 1. W. W. against a determined effort of mine owners, lumber barons and employment agenes sharks to suppress it. Railroad men in the Wall Street district are looking for a big light on the part of their employes for a advance of 12 per cent and also an unform wage schedule which they expect will be submitted to them in a few days. One of the officials of a large western system was quoted as saying that the rising prices of commodi ties is responsible for the demand for higher wages. It is his opinion that such a move would receive support from the public. At the same time, however, the railroads are being fogced to pay higher prices for materials, especially steel, and if wages go up, it is his opinion that the roads will be forced to increase freight rates as the only way out. He does not look for the Interstate Commerce Commis sion to place any obstacle in their way, Carned foto rare brought before them officially. That the fight now on is a serious one may be learned from a bulletin posted on quite a few Stock Exchange houses during the past few days.. The bulletin says in part: "One of the officials of the railroad brotherhoods says: 'This is the greatest, concerted wage demand in the history of American railroading. We have learned a lesson from railroads, and are pooling our interests and going after all the roads at the same time, on the same basis, no more doing business with us as divided units, That's played out. It don't pay and the negotiations must be conducted in a busi ness like way, from now on. The railroads are doing, more business than ever before. The situation at Bridgeport, Ohio, where the Amalgamated Association is out on strike against the American Sheet and Tinplate Company, otherwise the steel trust, presents a close repetition of the events in New Castle, Pa., where the same organization is out on strike against the same company. Developments have come slower in Bridgeport, the striking union probably being a little stronger. But the same tacties have been employed of simply staying out, "sticking," and trying to keep others from entering the mills. Meanwhile the socalled independent mills, every one of them affiliated with, or dependant on, the steel trust, have kept grinding merrily away, supplying, temporarily, the market. On the part of the employers, too, the same taeties have been employed. The same calling for the militia when there was no serious trouble and the same bluff threats to move or dismantle the mills if the men didn't come to time. In all such struggles as these our sympa thies are, of course, entirely with the men. They are of us. We are with them. The prize fair Italy-lay before them. They would win it or perish in the attempt. They won it. But think of the prize that lies before the working class today! The prize is the world, but think of America alone! Here is a land boundless in extent capable of supporting a thousand times its present population in ease and luxury. If then the time limit was fixed too, how was a woman to stretch her in come? Thirty-two years of life as a wage slave had taught her to believe that there was just one factor in the economic problem which could be changed, the number of hours in a day's work. So she demanded her one right, the right to overwork. Years of struggling for profits, of fighting the battle of competition, had taught her employer, too, just one thing, that there was no possibility of profit except in buying cheaper and ever cheaper the labor that created his boxes. This employer stated that the amount he paid for labor, 240,000 a year, was disproportionate to. the business and should be cut. The master was thoroughly class con scious; he saw profits only in increasing the efforts and lowering the reward of the working class. The wage slave was trained to the master's point of view; she saw no profit for fierself except by increasing her master's profits. She knew very well that she could push her weary body a little farther, but she no more thought of claiming a part of that 860,000 profit than of touching the riches "laid up in heaven." On one thing master and slave were agreed, the sacredness of profits, but the master could see clearly in whose interest the system was inaintained, the slave toiled on blindly, asking only for a chance at the crumbs.
