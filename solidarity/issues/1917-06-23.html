THE LINE-UP FOR THE BIG FIGHT More and more is it becoming evident that the future alignment in the class-war will be somewhat different from what was expected by the classical sociologists. It is a struggle now between the powers of Capitalism, reinforced with its armies of retainers and supporters, and the dispossessed, the entirely propertyless slave class-between those who find life and comfort possible and those who find existence itself becom ing actually impossible under existing conditions. It is a struggle be tween the "haves" and the "have nots," or what Emile Zola termed the "fat" and the "lean." The new line-up is, of course, a part of the great and irrepressible conflict between the workers who produce everything and own nothing and the parasites who produce nothing and own everything. But the issue is not quite as clear-cut as some of our well-meaning dogmatists would have us believe. The old grouping, of the capitalist class on one side and the working class on the other, with the middle class (petit bourgeoisie) sandwiched in the middle, has just about been broken up. While it is true that the middle class has not been entirely absorbed by the working class, as was expected, still it is out of the running as far as the big scrap is concerned. And the important fact is that the new middle class is that part of the working class which has been "bought out" by the besses-the highly paid specialists of industry, efficiency experts, pacemakers, straw-bosses and bulldozers, as well as the majority of skilled workers in nearly all industries. These are the men who are replacing the old middle class, and they are far more reactionary than the old "cockroach" element ever daged to be. They are what the I. W. W. have termed "scissorbills;" and they will fight as hard to keep Capitalism from going to its inevitable downfall as the parasites themselves. Like the shopkeepers who keep a close watch on the profit and loss columns and are willing to let things take their course or the land own ers of the "big sticks" who contemplate with satisfaction their broad and prolific acres, these working class props of Parasitism are content to hang onto their remunerative jobs and let the system that is crushing their less skilled and less fortunate brothers continue its deadly work in definitely. Cut off from the main current of the class struggle, what ever discontent they may have is of a purely local or craft nature, and it finds expression in either the petty reform of the ballot box or the aristocratic and exclusive trade union hall. anytiment is no longer a dynamic force in the labor movement. but revolutionary in its feeble opposition to Capitalism because all of its efforts are directed to the end that it can make its position under Capitalism more comfortable and secure. It is from this quagmire of reaction that the A. F. of L. and the reform Socialists draw never vitally function in the class war because its only relationship to industry is a parasitical one. But the artistocrats of labor with their select "unions" what Jack London called the "favored unions," represent the real cement that is being used to keep the crumbling system of Capitalist misrule from falling apart.
