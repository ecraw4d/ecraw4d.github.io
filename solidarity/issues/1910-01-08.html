Strike in New.Castle The strike of the tin workers at New Castle still drags along. About two-thirds of the mills are still operated by scabs, not so effectually of course as before the strike, but the scabs are learning the trade and becoming more efficient as time goes on. Tin is being turned out throughout the Shenango valley, not in such great quan tity or of so good quality as when the former employes were in charge, but still in sufficient quantity to keep the market well supplied, and the officials and stockholders of the American Sheet and Tinplate Company are letting the strikers do all the worrying. It is impossible not to admire the persistency of the strikers or toeplore the futility of their methods and the conservatism and timidity of the Advisory Board, and, in short, of all the officials of the A. A. and the A. F. of L. Natural outcome, however, of craft union methods. As evidence of the power of the modern trust and the inefficienes of craft unionism to give battle it needs only to be mentioned that the rules and regulations issued against the strikers by the municipal authorities who are of course the tools of the trust, have become daily more oppressive. Last Sunday a mass indignation meeting was held by the strikers to protest against the imprisonment of 27 of their number who were jailed three weeks previously for walking on the streets in a part of the city where the sherif had forbidden strikers to congregate. Among others the editor of Solidarity. also spoke by special invitation. The main point he tried to make was that indignation without power back of it was always ridiculous, and that the working class could only gain the power which would force the ruling class to treat their demands with respect by abandoning alike craft union tactics and all reliance on law courts and organizing industrially to take power into their own hands. Judging by the applause with which this position was greeted the lessons of the strike have not been lost on the tin workers of New Castle. Nor, did he forget to point out that the. indignation meeting should have been held when first the iron hand of injustice had been laid on their fellow workers, and not when their jail sentences had nearly expired... The Weight-Limit---and Others. Nineteen men were fired at the Carbondale, Pa. shops of the D. & H. R. R. They weighed less than 150 pounds, which, the company holds, is necessary in order to make employes strong enough to perform the daily labor required of them. That so large a number of men in one establishment should prove under weight appears to excite no comment. Yet it is a matter of no mean importance. It is one of the counfless straws indicating the physical deterioration of the American working class. It is another evidence of the fact that capitalism pays wages insufficient for the proper maintenance of the labor necessary for its profit. As such it makes plain once more, the necessity for the American working class to get together on industrial lines, in order to put a limit to the downward tendencies of capitalism, so that we may finally abolish it altogether. To the age limit, and to the weight limit, let us add the workers' limit of capitalism, as a prelude to the workers abolition of the same. Then there will be some limit ing done in the interests of the workers and not against them, as at present. The I. W. W. is a means to the end. Pinchot's Radicalism. The New York Call takes Pinchot to task for his recent radical address in an admirable manner. To his lamentations, against the influence of corporations in politics and the popular toleration of the same, it interposes the objection that, under capitalism, it was inevitable and no fault of the people. Says the Call: "Given great corporations their influence in every sphere of life must become tremendous. Given business it cannot be kept out of polities." This is good doctrine; it shows economics to be the determining and the controlling factor in politics. Nevertheless the Call does not apply this doctrine to the affairs of labor. It believes that polities determines economies. When labor is hit with a policeman's club, enjoined, or defeated in a strike, the Call refrains from urging the organization of labor's economic power on correct lines. Instead it clamors for a political party of labor, regardless of its economic form of organization or power. There can be no peace so long as hunger and want are found among millions of working people and the few, who make up the employing class, have all the good things of life. Between these two classes a struggle must go on until the workers of the world organize as a class, take possession of the earth and the machinery of production, and abolish the wage system. We find that the centering of the management of industries into fewer and fewer hands makes the trade unions unable to cope with the ever-growing power of the employing class. The trade unions foster a state of affairs which allows one set of workers to be pitted against another set of workers in the same industry, thereby helping defeat one another in wage wars. Moreover, the trade unions aid the employing class to mislead the workers into the belief that the working class have interests in common with their employers. These conditions can be changed and the interest of the working class upheld only by an organization formed in such a way that all its members in any one industry, or in all industries if necessary, cease work whenever a strike or lockout is on in any department thereof, thus making an injury to one an injury to all. It is the historic mission of the working class to do away with capitalism.
