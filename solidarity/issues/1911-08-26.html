This drunk had been led away from the crowd several times, no uniform police being around, and each time was brought back by oppo nents of the 1. W. W. Upon being led away for the fourth time he was advised to return by one Dr. Hamilton of the Vie tor Hotels a cheap lodging house just ad joining the corner of 18th and Larimer streets. Upon returning and re-commeneing the disturbance the drunk was taken across the street by fellow worker Carl Rave In crossing the street car tracks, the drunk, being already heavily overloaded, stumbled and fell into the adjoin ing gutter. Dr. Hamilton immediately secured an officer, several appeared as if by magic, and bad Rave arrested claiming be struck the man. Fellow Worker Smith ceased speaking and crossing the street, offered his services as a witness. An officer grabb d him making the remark "Witness nothing! You come along too!" Fellow Worker Mrs. Smith told the officer to take her and baby into custody, claiming she was guilty if her husband was. The officer refused to do this. Rave and Smith were then taken to the city jail, carefully searched. for dynamite and dangerous weapons, and were thrown into the taak. They were kept ex-communicado, no one being allowed to see them, no messages allowed to be transmitted, and all offers to bail turned down by the police officers in charge. The cell into which they were thrown had sleeping accommodations for less than a dozen peo ple, but the prisoners were 28 in number. In this filthy, unsanitary, foul smelling, yermin infested hole the men were forced to sleep without any blankets or bedding whatsoever, in company with drunks in every stage of filth. One man in particular had such a ster.ch coming from his person that even the strong nosed jailers. were forced to make ham take a bath in the morning. ODERN INDUSTRIALISM BY JUSTUS EBERT A Series of Descriptive Articles Written Especially for Solidarity." ARTICLE THREE The Extractive Industries. Modern industrialism is a machine of many parts. It has various forms and combinations of activities. All dovetail into each other and either stand or fall together. Great strikes and industrial panics serve to illustrate their interdependence. Lands, railroads, factories, warehouses, and banks form the successive units in the modern industrial process. In other words, there exist extractive, transporting, manufacturing and commercial industries, all capped by high finance. These constitute the broad, inter related subdivisions of modern industrialism The extractive industries are the primary industries. They extract food and raw material from land and sea. They include farming, fishing, lumbering and mining. Without them, society would be impossible; progress a word unbeard. Social bodies cannot live and flourish without the cereals, meats, cottons and woolens of the farm, and the fish-foods and by-products of the waters, wherewith to feed and clothe humanity; the timber and mineral products of the earth, wherewith to build the humble cottage and the costly sky scraper, and the fuel wherewith to give them both light and warinth. Nor can they erect and operate immense industrial establishments, railroads and ships; or carry on the varied activities that supply the material basis of the intellectual, artistic and spiritual life of the day. The conservation movement is a recognition of the importance of the extractive industries and of the material basis on which society, flourishes in its most highly developed phases. Hence its far-reaching agitation and influence. Farming, fishing, lumbering and mining all exhibit the same general characteristics and tendencies. They are all operated in direct connection with other industries, on the lines of the factory system, with company houses, stores and other labor exs ploiting adjunets unknown to the latter. In the case of lumbering and mining, the inter-relation to other industries was well shown in the first article of this series. Therein facts were cited from annual re ports showing the laternational Harvester Co. (the harvester trust) to be the owner and lessee of vast tracts of timber, coal. and iron ore lands in seven states. These It exploits by means of mines, sawmills, railroads and all the industrial equipment peculiar to lumbering and mining. The same thing holds true of the United States Steel Corporation, which was also cited in fue same article, The steel, trust was therein shown to be the owner of various kinds of lands, all of which it exploited, in direct connection with its steel and iron mills, in the most modern fashion. And what holds true of these two trusts, holds true also of many others. The International Paper Co. (the paper trust), as is well known, is the owner of vast tracts of timber lands in the Adirondacks, New England and the South, which are exploited in direct co-operation with its paper mills. Other companies, notably that backed by the Hearst newspaper interests, own and control large timber tracts at home and in Canada. These are necessary to a cheap and abundant supply of wood palp and paper, unhampered by market conditions or monopolistic control. With many railroads, the ownership of timber lands is necessary to insure a steady supply of ties, poles, etc. Lead pencil companies, wagon companies, car construction companies, furniture companies-all have their own timber lands to draw on for raw supplies, When timber lands are not directly owned and exploited in co-operation with other industries by one and the same corporation or trust, they are owned and exploited indirectly through large interests and combinations. The Weyerhausen in terests, the largest timber land owners in the country, the head of which is repated to be wealthier than Rockefeller, operate saw mills, industrial railroads,etc., throughout the Northwest and British Columbia under a variety of corporate names and in alliance with the Hill trunk lines In the Southwest, the Saw Mill Operators Association, a $100,000,000 combination, controls 300 mills and the lumber industry of the Southwest generally. Another big combination is the Yellow Pine Manufac turer's Association, with headquarters at St. Louis, Mo. These large interests and combinations are the great shingle, sash, door, blind and other building material furnishers of this country. When the building industry of the country becomes more concentrated their relations to the building industry will become more direct(1). In coal mining, as already indicated, in the reports of the Harvester and Steel trusts, the same conditions prevail as in timbering. Coal is produced by various corporations in direct co-operation with, other industries, owned and operated by them. In the bituminous district, to speak of the Pittsburg Coal and Iron Co., is to speak of the steel trust, of which it is a subsidiary. The same is trapith the Con nellsville coke district, where the Henry C. Frick Coal and Coke Co. reigns in the interests of the steel trust. In the West, the Colorado Fuel and Irons Co., a giant teel and iron corporation, also a giant coal producer, the biggest in the Rocky Mountain territory. In the anthracite region. the Temple Iron and Coal Co., is synonymous with the seven coal carrying railroads, which own and control it, making of it the great anthracite coal trust that it is.(2) In the Westmoreland, Pa., and West Virginia coal fields, the Pennsylvania R. R. Co. is greatly interested. The railroads in general are great owners of natural resources. They require their raw materials; the railroads being great consumers of coal of all kinds. They also need the traffic their transportation produces. These insure greater stability and profits to the railroads. In fact, it is difficult to separate coal, iron, steel, oil (another natural product) and the railroads. They are inextricably bound together and as difficult to separate as it is to untie the mythological Gordian knot.(3) In metal mining, it is almost the same as in coal and iron ore mining. The harvester and steel trusts, as already shown, mine immense iron ore deposits in the Northwest. The relations of the copper and gold mines, smelters, railroads, warehouses and offices of the Guggenheim interests, whether in New or old Mexico, for instance, are so direct and close that it is difficult to tell where the one begins and the other ends. Here's a news de spatch that illustrates and clinches the point: "Monterey, Mexico, Aug 13.-Announcement was made at the offices of the American Smelting and Refining Company here today of the purchase by that company of the Tiro General mines at Charcos, State of San Luis Potosi, and the Central Potosi Railroad, eight miles long, which connects the mines with the National Rail ways of Mexico." The interdependence is complete; no matter from which end the process is viewed. The same holds true of the lead zinc mines of Missouri, which are the property of big industrial and railroad corporations. The same chain of allied tech nical processes and interests bind them close together, despite anti-combination and other futile laws. (4). Farming, The Modern "Peculiar Institution." Now, while the close inter-relation of lumbering and mining with other industries is easy to establiski, a little difficulty will be experienced when it comes to show the same with reference to farming. Ow ing to its belated development, arming is held by many to be the modern "peculiar institution." It is said. to be outside of the path of industrial evolution, with conditions peculiar to itself. Hence, many who are otherwise gifted with a sense of humor, believe social progress should be in the small farmer's interests and from the viewpoint of his property rights. In other words, the small farmer tail should wag the social dog (5). This view is, of course, erroneous. Farm ing tends toward industrial forms, in close co operation with industry in general. In the first place, one has but to note the increasing specialization of farming to become convinced of this tendency. This specialization makes possible the development of farming on lines paralleling industrial de velopment in general. It admits of specialized machinery with specialized labor and the large capital that operates in unison with both and is made necessary by them. Take, for instance, dairying, with its cheese and butter-making and milk bottling factories; its staff of laborers and executives; its pastures and cows in the SOLIDARITY driven machines and factories operated by wage slaves, are being organized tor farmers by farmers. Like the manufacturers in the days of the pool and the syndicate, the farmers are primarily consolidating to take care of outlets and markets; they do not consolidate in order to farm together.(6) But consolidated farming will come as sure as consolidated manufacture followed the pool and syndicate. And for precisely the same reason, viz: the development of conditions within soil production itself that will make such a course unavoidable. History repeats itself; even in the modern "peculiar institution." The Trend To Large, Corporate Farming. A factor to this end is the increasing size of farms. From 1850 to 1880 inclusive the average number of acres to a farm decreased from 202.6 to 183.7. This, too, despite the presence of "bonanza" or large intensive farming. In 1900, with small or intensive farming the rule, the average number of acres to a farm had risen to 146.2 acres. Again in 1850, the value of the average farm was $2,786; in 1900, $3,562.(7). This tendency in size and valuation is likely to increase; the use of machinery demands a larger area of cultivation for profitable exploitation. Other conditions making for consolidated farming, are manifesting themselves in a variety of ways. First, in the large, scien tifically managed, specialized and consolidated farm now evolving, such as the Barber farm at Barberton, Ohio.(8). A farm of this character necessitates a revolution that can only be met by large capital. Large capital is today secured on the basis "of the increased earning arising out of consolidation. A scientifically managed, specialized and consolidated farm is a paying farm. Second, in the cultivation of the soil by big corporations directly dependent on it for supplies; or that are going into farming on a more comprehensive basis for the profit there is in it. The first represent food, canning, preserving, tobacco and cotton interests. They seek sources of supplies independent of speculative or mar ket influenees; or they seek to modify latter in their own interests by means of their own operations. The second seek enrichment by combining many industries primarily dependent upon the soil into one harmonious process. Hence, the breakfast food companies are interested in, the production of cereals on a large scale; the preserving and canning companies, such as the Lipton Co. and the Lutz and Schram Co., are conducting truck gardens on an extensive plane; while, at the same time, the tobacco trust is purchasing lands for tobacco cultivation, and a recently organized textile combine is doing the same in the matter of cotton. When an army is organizing, are gen erals appointed for a term of one or two years? How, silly, They are appointed for war. I believe in electing officers, of course, bot. I don't believe in limiting an efficient officer to one or two terms for no other reason, so far as I can see, than be cause some politician has his eyes glued on a pie card and what more h can get. He wants to increase his chances by having more electious, shorter term.s To do this, he parades his dope under the guise of democracy, usually claiming this agita tion comes from the rank and file, ete. Conventions are better able to determine the caliber of officers, than all the referendums ever conceived. I think the members of the L. W. W. are the kind, when they see anything wrong, they fix it. They were not long in putting the boots to Sherman, MeCabe, et al., at the second convention; and say, will some of the referendum friends tell how they would do the same trick with their never failing rum dum? When an officer has to stand before live men and explain his acts, seems to me that will in sure better service than to have him send it out over 100,000 miles for setion and the action to be taken over another 100, 000 miles of red or pink tape. I am one of those that want the works and I am not so dammed particular how I get it, but I want it as soon as 1 cân. We are not electing officers to honor them or because we feel sorry for them, but because they can and are delivering the goods.
