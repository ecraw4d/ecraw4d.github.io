The Indianapolis Situation Following the violent series of events, and the tame ending of the recent street car workers' strike in Indianapolis, the organized association of employers forced the resignation of heels of the big money powers of all nations, harrassing and annoying them in the assertion of their new found growth. But there is a day of reckoning near at hand. From various quarters comes the words so ominous to small enterprisebankruptcy and liquidation. Lord Milner, in a recent address before an English banking institute, intimated that this process is now world-wide and that owing to "the scramble for capital" it will increase in intensity and be capable of resistance by only the most powerful and best managed corporations. In other words, we may shortly expect to see on a world-wide scale, such a squeezing out of the petit bourgeois, as was never seen before. And conversely, we may shortly expect to see on a world-wide scale such a triumph of the large combinations of capital, as was never seen before it will be a case of the survival of the fittest; of the greater body attracting unto itself and dominating the Of course such a process of confiscation for such it really is cannot proceed without some consequences to the working class working class has been confronted with an increase in the cost of living amounting to sixty-five cent since 1897. This increase has not been met with a corresponding increase in wages and has been productive of much discontent and revolt as a consequence. Now, with the prospect of a world-wide panic facing us, in addition, is there any reason to believe that this writer has before him the Nov. 26 "Market Letter" of the stock discontent and revolt will decrease?
