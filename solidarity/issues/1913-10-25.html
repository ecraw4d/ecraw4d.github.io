THE "MIXED" LOCAL IN THE WEST In our last issue under the sub-heading "The Mixed Local," we considered some aspects of that peculiar formation, as illustrated only in the eastern section of the country. Our conclusions from the facts at hand were, that the "mixed" local had proven itself to be a failure as a recruiting body, and a very inefficient instrument for propoganda. A previous editor ial on the same subject attracted the attention of Editor Cook of the Los Angeles "Wooden Shoe," who says with reference the West. But something vital is lacking: a disposition on the part of the tens of thousands of recruits made in this way, to stick to the organization. We noted a similar defect in the East, due to incorrect formation of the industrial unions as relating to the branches thereof. It will not do to insist that these men left the I. W. W. because they "were not revolutionary." It were far better to ask, Why did the I. W. W. not hold a larger percentage of them, at least, until they became revolutionary? We think the answer is to be found in the structure and funetioning of the "mixed" local itself. When these workers left the distributing centers and landed on the job, they found that the I. W. W. did not follow them. They lost their connection between the job and the union. There was no "union on the job"; the union was in the city perhaps a hundred miles away. The "camp delegate" system in some sections, for the collection of dues and conveying of information, is a step in the direction of overcoming this defect, but is not sufficient. The men must be definitely organized on the job, through the branch formation provided by the I. W. W. constitution. Take, for example, the redwood lumber belt. The writer spent a year there, in Humboldt county, California. At that time (1906) the A. F. of L. had five local unions of woodsmen and sawmills workers, in as many different sections of the county. The I. W. W. had ONE local, with headquarters in Eureka, without any branch formation. Workers in camps, sometimes 20 or more miles from Eureka, would frequently ask the writer: "Why don't you form a local here; these men won't go to town to local meeting." Of course we couldn't see it that way; as "one big union" was all we wanted. It never occurred to us that there is more than one way to form "one union." That these lumber workers could be organized on the job, in branches, meeting regularly, discussing their own job conditions, sending delegates to a central committee of the local in Eureka, and thereby receiving communications from other branches throughout the industry. In this way they would feel that they were a vital part of the union, and the idea of industrial solidarity would grow in proportion as the organization developed. We failed to perceive this vital fact at the time; and, following the strike of 1907, in which the A. F. of L. led the woodsmen and sawmill workers to defeat, both organizations disappeared from that section for the time being. Reorganization along the same "mass" lines will hardly have any other result in the future. The fact of locals "disappearing" from season to season, need make no difference in this respect; they should be correctly formed at least while the season lasts. Transfers are easy, from one industrial union to another, as the worker shifts from one locality to another, and from one industry to another.
