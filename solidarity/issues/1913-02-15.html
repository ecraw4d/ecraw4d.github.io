To Friends of Freedom Everywhere- This is the old, old story repeated once more. The workers, without any other means at their disposal than their own organisation and solidarity, on one side, and the employers, armed with all the powers of government and money, on the other. In 1908 the dock workers of Sweden either struck against intolerable working conditions, or were locked out by the ship owners, I do not now remember which. As both the workers and the bosses were well organised and prepared for war, a terrible struggle soon followed. The soll darity of the Swedish workers, being developed as perhaps nowhere else, it be came practically impossible to obtain strike breakers to take the jobs of the men on strike. The result was that shipping and foreign commerce were almost paralysed. The workers were jubilant. But this could not continue. When the ship owners could not get strike breakers from within the country or from any of the Scandinavian countries they began to look for them elsewhere. They advertised and searched everywhere Finally they succeeded in recruiting several hundred jobless and miserable creatures at some of the English seaports. These were shipped in a vessel called the Amalthea to the city of Malmo, one of the southernmost parts of Sweden. This action aroused the community to such a degree that these strike breakers could not be put ashore for fear that violence would be used against them. Be- During the day they worked at the docks under protection, and at night they were brought back to their floating quarters. In the meantime the organized Swedish dock workers walked the streets, they and their families starving. It was under these circumstances that three young men, Nelson, Roseberg and Stern, not even members of the dock workers' union, deelded that something must be done to get these strike breakers away from the shores of Sweden. And this is what they decided to do: In the loneliness of the night, under cover of darkness, these men, the oldest of whom was not yet 26, equipped themselves with a dynamite bomband in a small boat approached the Amalthes which lay anchored in the harbor with its cargo of strike breakers, and placed the bomb on its deck, lit the fuse and departed. An explosion followed, killing one strike breaker, slightly injuring a few, and badly frightening all. The entire machinery of the law was now put in motion. Nelson and Roseberg were sentenced to die, and Stern to life imprisonment. Later, Nelson and Roseberg were "pardoned," and given life terms also. According to their own statements the men did not intend to blow up the ship or to kill any of the strike breakers, but simply to frighten them so that they would no longer.remain at work. Be that as it may, these men cannot by any fairminded person be considered as dangerous criminals who should be kept in the dungeons for life. The enlightened workers of Sweden at this time feel that these men have suffered enough, and are desides, no one in the city would have served manding that they be released. FRENCH SYNDICALISM By LEON JOUHAUX, Sec'y C. G. T. of France. (Address delivered at the People's Hall, Brussels, Belgium, Dec 6, 1911. Translated for Solidarity by Herbert Sturges, Painesville, Ohio.) (Continued From No. 168) "Revolutionary" and "Reformist." The ideas which have directed the up ward march of syndicalism have, because of their radiating power, penetrated everywhere and created a general disturbance. All classes of our society have gradually come to feel the influence of syndicalist ideas. The ferocious repression now exercised by the authorities against organised labor has no other source but in the hatred of the bourgeoisie for a movement it recognizes to be its superior, and which it feels to be bringing the fruitful ferments of the future revolution and the elements of. transformation; the hatred of a class which has failed in its historic mission and sees in the working class, organised industrially, the force capable of taking up this his torie mission and carrying it out to a successful conclusion. And now let us enter upon the discus sion of the most delicate subject, that of the examination of the two tendencies which dispute for the leadership of the la bor movement. Our labor movement is traversed by two currents of ideas which are spoken of by the terms "revolutionary" and "reformist." First of all let us destroy a legend which has too long found credit among foreigu syndicalists: "The C. G. T. is directed in a revolutionary way in spite of the majority of its members being reformists." That is a profound mistake; for over and above the fact that the great majority of the federations are revolutionary, it must not be forgotten that in the federations called reformist there are large revolutionary minorities. To be convinced of that you have only to read over the reports of the congresses of these different organiza tions: Printing, textile, lighting, etc. Even if proportional representation were applied, as our opponents of the other tendency demand, for all that, the diree tion of the C. G. T. would be unchanged. The spirit of revolution shows itself not only in deeds, but also in thought. To destroy the revolutionary current it would be necessary to overturn the confederal statutes which are thoroughly impregnated with this virile current. The boldness of the thought and idea determines the energy of action. Moreover, it is not at this moment when the state founders inte the most lamentable weakness and bankruptey that our reformist comrades, wishing above ail to introduce the syndicalist movement within the pale of the state, can hope to see the triumph of their ideas. The resolutions adopted at the last congress of Toulouse and at the special conventions of on, when we have realized the social transformation, we will then establish friendly relations and they will be fruit ful, for they will be based on harmony of interests and not on antagonism. The C. G. T. Anti-Parliamentarian "The anti-parliamentarian action of the C. G. T." has often been spoken of. Is it quite true that it is anti-parliamentary setion, in the sense ordinarily given this word? To be that it would require the confederal organization to take up a position in the electoral struggle, a thing which it has never done. Its "anti-parliamentarism" consists simply in bolding itself at a distance from those in authority and in demanding from officeholders an absolute neutrality in its internal organisation and in its activity. It is non-parliamentary. When a retorm of general nature is needed, instead of addressing the public authorities we have recourse to activity outside the walls of parlisment to cause pressure on the legislature We seek first to set up a current of public opinion and a state of affairs which render the reform inevitable. In this way the members of the syndicats are always conscious that reforms, whatever they are, can only be the results of their efforts. It is thus that the passing of the laws secured was upon industrial accidents, employment bureaus, and weekly rest. And even today these laws are not enforced except where labor organizations sare sufficlently powerful and have life enough to enforce their enforcement. Take, for instance, the law of industrial accidents. which, thanks to class jurisprudence, has undergone such profound modifications that its original provisions no longer exist. Unless the injured worker has a permanent disability of more than 5 per cent he has no right to an income. In the metal shops of Meurthe and Moselle the syndi calist organization is unable to get a footing because of the expulsion of foreign workmen; the law has never been enforced. As to the employment buresus and the weekly rest, it is the same. We have had fellow workers condemned for demonstrations demanding the enforcement of these laws The Struggle Against the State In the same way as syndicalism, struggles against the oppression of employers it struggles against the state. We know that while the authority of the state exists no serious change can be brought about. We also deny the possibility that the state which governs us and pretends to be republican should set up a just balance among the multiple and complex interests Paris prove this. Never had the proposi which arise in our country as in every tions of the Confederation been accepted with such unanimity. This congress and these conventions have realized almost entirely, not moral unity, which is unrealizable, but unity of action. On the other hand, if by "reformist" you mean partisans of reform, we are all that; for the French labor movement does not oppose reforms and ameliorations, but, on the contrary, aims to obtain the maximum of reforms. But it wants to distinguish the good from the bad. It is about the means to employ for reaching these results that we disagree. So when our reformist comrades wish to form alliances between workers and em ployers for obtaining the ameliorations demanded, we declare, on the contrary, that this method can bring no effective result. For if the advantages thus obtained are not illusory, reseting against the workers, they will be fastened upon the baeks of the consumers, of which the workers are the great majority. To our minds there are présent in the labor struggle two opposed interests which are irreconcilable, and the advantages to be gained must be by the victory of one of the two contestants. It is up to the workers to create a strike technique, taking advantage of every instruction from daily events. For this task the militant revolutionists have put themselves in harness. Without action a movement must inevitably disintegrate and die of atrophy. Action is life, as well as the power which brings victory. We accustom the workers to create for themselves this power which they now need and which they will need in the future As long as we are under the capitalist regime we ought to battle against the oppressive regime. Our slogan must be: "Live to fight." Later modern society. Its constitution and its foundations make of it the defender of the rights of the bourgeoisie against whom we are in conflict. It is the organism of domination of a hostile class. It represents the superannuated forms of the past adapted to the present. A simple change tomorrow in the governmental label, which should come from the same spirit, would bring no serious improvement in the condition of the exploited. Based upon respect for pirvate property, the State can only be the enemy of the working class, which desires the suppression of this form of property and its replacement by the inauguration of communism. We have broken with the past; but we have equally no intention of freezing up forever in the present; we march towards the future, and in our way stands the bourgeois state. Whether we want to or not, then, we have to join battle with it, since there it is and in the name of the law 'akes it upon itself to prevent us from passing. We do not carry on this fight by sending representatives to the deliberatiye assemblies. We conduct it through our organizations by raising the moral level of the workers and by creating class-consciousness. The bigger this consciousness becomes the more the authority of the state will lessen, and the day when this consciousness flowers out completely the authority of the state will be reduced to sero. That is the goal we aim at; that is why it has been very correctly said that "the C. G. T. is forming a state within the state."" Anti-militarism Anti-militarists we are from a two-fold POLIDARIVY point of view. First, because the army comes into all the conflicts of labor and capital for the latter's benest. Does not the army take the workers' places when they strike for better conditions? They make the workers in uniform play the part of scabe. The army is designed much more for the satisfaction of the spirit of lucre and rapine of the capitalists, and much more for the defense of bourgeois privileges against the workers, than for the safeguarding of national interests. Against this infernal infernal intervention we should fortify ourselves. Events such as those of Limoges, Raon-l'Etape, Narbonne and Villeneuve- Saint-George should not be repeated. Workers who have become soldiers should be able in every circumstance to obey their conscience and refuse to act as scabs or assassins of the people, which their efficers sometimes command them to do, speaking in the name of a regime of hatred and oppression. As a renegade of the Socialist Party once said: "Bayonets and guns should become intelligent." To this end we have established in every syndical organization treasuries of "the soldiers' penny," (sou du soldat) now being prosesented in the persons of three militant workers. These institututions have the purpose of preserving between the worker while he is in the barracks and his organization the continuous relations indispensable for keeping up in our young friends when they return from service a state of mind favorable to the demands of labor. And in the second place, since we sincerely desire the coming of a better soci ety, and since this cannot be realized without the co-operation of the army, we should remain anti-militarist facts just related no longer existed. The army is a means of servatude; it creates passivity, resignation and disolute habits; it works for the physical degeneration of the race through the contamination, by alcohol, syphilis and fevers, of the healthy bodies it takes for its murderous purposes. We who desire strong wills and robust bodies are profoundly and sincerely against the army. Anti-Patriotism We are anti-patriots and deny the utility of wars of defense, or rather of pseudo-defense, even us we deny that of wars of conquest. We apply the two principles of the first Inbor international: "The workers have no country;" "Workers of all countries, unite!" How can we conceive the idea of country, we who possess absolutely nothing, we who are obliged to fight to defend the right of existence? The concept of country is for those who can to a certain extent satisfy their desires and meet their needs. Office holders, members of parliament, financiers, manufacturers, landlords, stockholders, etc., are right in proclaiming the existence of countries, for their disappearatice would toll the knell of their privileges. And the enlarging of what they call the national patrimony is for them in reality the possi bility of enlarging the field of their exploitation and increasing this source of their income. Keeping nations in a state of hostility and encouraging this stupid hatred among among nations and races are for them the surest way of preserving and perpetuating the present state of society of which they are the sole beneficiaries. As for the workers, whichever way they turn, they behold only the property of their exploiters. No bit of land, no industrial equipment to which they have a right. Under these conditions why should they have the simplicity to get themselves killed for interests which are not theirs? But, say the patriots, a country is more than that; there are still its traditions, its manners and customs, etc. To which we reply: "Well then your countries are themselves subdivided into an infinite number of little countries, for every distriet has its own manners, customs and traditions, and sometimes its own lan guage." To all these self-interested lies we answer: "The worker's country is the whole world where he can eat, drink, love, sleep and think freely. And you patriots cannot get this country for us." No, the enemies of French workmen are not outside the frontiers; they are inside. Our enemy? It is our master! On the other hand the profound transformation which we call for must be international and not merely national. So we ardently desire closer relations and more solidarity among the various proletariats of the world. Patriotism also develops war-like sentiments, and we are against war. THE HOTEL WORKERS' STRIKE Dy Frank Penn The strike in New York City, of the International Hotel Workers' Union, has been called off. While the press has not credited the strikers with winning any thing, there has been a distinct gain for the workers in this industry. In a large number of hotels and restaurants the conditions existing prior to the strike have been somewhat ameliorated. The 10-hour day has been established in some ploces, Pay for overtime work and an increased wage for numerous workers has been secured. The fining system has received a severe jolt; notably in the "Child's" restaurants, where the workers were not even on strike. Better food has been served since the beginning of the strike in many places where the workers had not then walked out. Chief amongst their gains is perhaps, the fact that the militants have become known to one another, and in the next strike will be able to do the team work so essential to victory. That the strike was not a complete suecess is due to numerous causes, chief among which were the inexperience of the strikers and their lack of preparation. There were no aggressive executive-minded men to formulate the plans which might have carried the strikers to success. Or, if there were, they had not grouped themselves as such, but lost their energies in individualistic activities. Only too late, toward the very end, did these men appear to realise the necessity of their coming together upon the basis of their common determinations and their common understanding. It is to be hoped that they will have learned the lesson that organized effort accomplishes more than any other kind. From the first moment our organisers, Fellow Workers Egerman, Flynn, Tresca and Trautmann, arrived, they were overwhelmed with a mass of detail work that should have been left to the attention of sub-committees. The strike was already on when they arrived. There was no time to put it upon an organised basis, as these fellow workers were thrust into the brunt of the battle, where speech-making, publicity work, executive work of a great variety, the marshalling of pickets and all the variations of labor incident to a strike, was expected of them. They did the best they could under the circumstonces. There was a large element of conservative hostility to their neivities, that did not help toward the winning of the strike. They were frequently subjected to errest or assault. Whenever this happened it was a decided drawback to whatever advance the strikers were making, for the reason that the strike management was largely in their bands, and absence from the battleground spells defeat. The capitalist press abruptly changed its tactics of partial fairness toward the strike, and one morning, three days before the strike was called off, it announced that the strikers were defeated. It did more; it "scare-headed" its columns with this lie: "Waiters Scurry for Jobs; Strike Broken." At that time the strik ers were holding very firm and it looked like certain victory. But many men whose unionism was of feeble quality, when they read their morning paper, believed the above, and went to work without first consulting headquarters as to whether the strike was over or not. Further confusion was created by the fact that many of the bosses were willing to sign the demands presented them. The waiters were the ones most affected by this. It is easily understood though, when one recalls, that theirs is largely a daily wage. Each day's wage is received in large part through tips. Because of this the average waiter is less likely to have a reserve fund than others in this industry. This proves the necessity of the waiters abolishing the tipping system and placing themselves on regular wage standard if they would be in a position to fight their masters as other workers are fighting. The lesson of solidarity has not been taught the workers of this industry. Absurd as it may seem, there were many strikers who could not see why they should not return to work where their bosses were willing or anxious to sign up. The general strike was called for the express purpose of helping those who were out for nearly three weeks; it being expected that the withdrawal of all would be sufficient pressure to bring the New York Hotel Owners' Association to quick terms. And so the self-imposed discipline of solidarity not being general enough, many of the strikers held shop meetings, with the intent of returning to work whether the general strike was called or It was soon realised that the best thing for the International Hotel Workers' Unton would be to call off the strike This was done by the rank and file in mass meeting Such houses as have either locked all unionists out, or have taken them back without granting the new conditions demanded, are to be subjected to the attentions of those militants amongst the strikers whose memories are keenest, and whose class intentions are the most determined. This strike is but another illustration of the fact that withdrawal of labor power, mass picketing and speeches, alone cannot win a strike. In no other industry, with the exception of transportation, which also has to do with immediate-consumption goods, can the workers win their battles with such comparativ ease. In this indus try there is a daily demand The commodity which the workers handle can be made subject to a regiment of sabotage peculiar to no other industry. The housing of guests can become so impossible through the same practices that the employers will hasten to grant concessions. Their profits, being like the waiters' wage, a, daily return, are subject to immediate depletion at the hands of an aggressive hotel workers' organization. A whole volume could be written on the perversities of men who prefer bending their heads before a rain of police clubs, to using their brains to dislocate the mechanism of their industry. Mass picketing has its value, but a little thinking. followed by action in another direction, will bring quicker and more lasting results. From now on it should be the task of eyery intelligent worker to study the mechanism of his industry, that he may know its most easily dislocated parts. Any wage worker, wishing to become a member of the Industrial Workers of the World, may proceed in the following nmanner: 1. If you live in a locality where there is a union of your industry or a mixed (recruiting) union already in existence, apply to the secretary of that local union. He will furnish you with an application blank containing the Preamble to the I. W. W. Constitution and the two questions which each candidate for admission must answer in the affirmative. The questions are as follows: "Do you agree to abide by the constitution and regulations of this organization?" "Will you diligently study its principles and make vourself acquainted with its purposes?!" The initiation fee is fixed by the Local Union, but cannot be more than $5.00 in any instance, and is usually $1.00 or less. The monthl dues cannot exceed $1.00 and are in most locals from 35 to 50 cents 2. If there is no Local Union of the I. W. W. in your vicinity, you may become a Member-at-Large by making application to the General Secretary, whose address is given below, You will be required to answer affirmatively the two above questions, and pay an initiation fee of $2.00. The monthly dues see $1.00 for Members-at-Large. 8. Better still, write to the General Secretary for a Charter Application Blank. Get no less than TWENTY signatures thereon, of bonafide wage workers in any one industry (for a Local Industrial Union) or in several industries (for a Local Recruiting or mixed Union) and send the charter application with the names to the General Secretary, with the $10.00 charter fee. Supplies, constitutions and instructions will then be sent you, and you can proceed to organize the local. Join the I. W. W. Do it now. The address of the General Secretary of the I. W.
