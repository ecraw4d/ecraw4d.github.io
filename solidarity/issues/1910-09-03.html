Trautmann, GENERAL EXECUTIVE BOARD J. J. Ettor, Chas. Scurlock, C. H. Axelson, Francis Miller, George Speed. THE STEEL TRUST AND THE UNIONS. A shout of triumph is due from the steel trust. It has conquered craft unionism. One after another of the unions which formerly possessed some power and influence have gone down before its migh ty onslaughts. The miners' union on the Mesaba Range in Minnesota; the Lake Seamen; the Amalgamated Association of Iron, Steel and Tin Workers have been shattered to pieces in the desperate struggle of the past ten years. Now the coal miners of the Pittsburg district organized in the United Mine Workel of America, are engaged in a life and death struggle with the same antagonist. To the casual and superficial observer, these craft union wrecks may appear as marking the end of all unionism among the slaves of the steel trust. To one who looks beneath the surface of things, and follows closely the trend of events and the shaping of conditions on the industrial field, these disasters to the old style unions only denote the clearing of the ground for the fertile growth of industrial unionism. Strikes in the past year have furnished ample ground for that conclusion. MeKees Rocks in 1909; Bethlehem at the beginning of the present year, and other smaller affairs, have shown the direction of the revolutionary wind. Here we saw no longer the skilled workers the aristocrats of the industry struggling with the giant corporation; but masses of unskilled, low paid and miserably exploited slaves break ing out in spontaneous revolts more far reaching in their influence and effects than any strike hitherto known. These revolts have their reasons rooted in the conditions of the steel industry itself. That industry has, so to say, been revolutionised many times in the past 20 years. Concentration has been the order of the day; skill of workers has been minimized or wiped out by the development of the machine process. The workers, hampered by the form and spirit of craft unions, have as yet been unable to adjust themselves to these revolutionary changes in the industry. As a consequence their wages have gone down in proportion as the pro-. fits and dividends of the steel trust have gone up; while working hours and conditions have become unbearable. Hence to the superficial observer, the case of the steel trust's employes now appears hopeless. But the unorganized revolts of the past year have shown that the slaven are not asleep. In fact industrial conditions will not permit them to sleep. The brutal and relentless exploiter the steel trust-is its own worst enemy. It is drunk with power. Having smashed the craft, unions, the trust smiles at the thought that it will ever have men may not do." This "learned judge" knows better than that. The fact is, that the very cloak manufacturers, for whom Judge Goff issued that learned document, were at that very moment combined against their workmen. And the great New York bankers and others interested were in the combination with them. What Judge Goff meant, but didn't say was that "what the employers MAY DO, the workmen MAY NOT DO." W. contention that the political state moves only in obedience to the interests of the ruling economic class. When in the course of events, the working class acquires the economic power through the organization and control of its social labor power in the industries, the political state will either be harmless in opposition to the workers' in dustrial power, or will move in obedience to the workers' interests. The point is further emphasized by the . W. W. strikers in San Diego. On the lookout for any and every weapon that might help them to win their fight for a shorter work day and increased wages, these strikers even consulted the law books, of California, and dug up an eight hour law passed in 1879, amended in 1905, and which has been a "dead letter" all those years. The law is explicit in reference to eight hours for all public work. It absolutely prohibits the working of men on public work over eight hours per day, and provides that the city's official agent, in charge of such work, can withhold from the contract price $10 per employe for cach day that each employe has been made to work more than eight hours by the contractor. If such agent or official fails to take cognizance of the violations of the law by the contractor or sub-contractor and proceed, accordingly he is guilty of a misdemeanor and the offense is punishable by a fine of not more than $500, or imprisonment of six months or both, at the discretion of the judge. The 1. W. W. men are striking for an eight hour day and $2.50 against the Barber Asphalt Co., on contract of laying pavesment for the city of San Diego. The company has been working the men 9 and 10 hours. Hence the strikers are after the city superintendent of streets as well as the company. The strike of the A. A. against the steel trust has been officially declared of. This strike has been going on for over a year. It began as a strike against the open shop order of the trust. This strike has been a lesson to the American working class, proving that craft unions and craft strikes are utterly helpless against the power of organized capital. It shows the futility of a small percentage of the workers in an industry attempting to carry on a successful batle against a mighty corporation. Here were a few skilled workers organized in a craft union putting up a battle for a closed shop, with the great majority of workers in the industry completely unorganized, and with the conditions of the industry in such a state as to make it an easy matter for these unorganized unskilled workers to be used by the corporation to break the strike. This vast army of workers, unrecognized by the craft union, remained at work and made it an easy matter for the trust to defeat and annihilate the A. A. This strike began on July 1, 1909, about two weeks before the big strike of the Pressed Steel Car workers at McKees Rocks. Yet the McKees Rocks-strikers in two months forced the Pressed Steel Car Company to come to terms. Why? Because all the workers of the plant were involved in the strike, thus closing down the plant completely. The A. A., using the fighting methods of craft unions, living on the glory of the past, allowed itself to be hampered by craft prejudices and traditions, and refuse ed to adapt itself to modern methods of fighting these industrial battles.. The A. A. is part of the A. F. of I Still we find that while the A. A. was fighting nobly against the trust the members of other craft unions also affiliated with the A. F. of L. remained at work, therestrike. by assisting the trust to defeat the men on The lesson of this strike is this: Modern industrial conditions demand modern fighting methods. The workers can no longer hope to fight their battles with out of date weapons. The workers in the tin mills must be organized in ONE BIG UNION, just as the mill owners are organized in ONE BIG CORPORATION. The only way to defeat the trust is to organize to control the labor power of the workers, which feed the trust in other words form a LABOR TRUST. Let us hope that the mill workers will profit by the lesson of this strike, and get into an organization which will enforce the closed shop rule with the organized power of all the workers in the industry. STORIES FROM REAL LIFE THE COSSACK. BY LOUIS DUCHEZ. "Tim" was a Cossack. I met him in New Castle. It was the first day the bunch of them arrived, about fifteen in number, to patrol the streets in the vicinity of the tin plate mills of the Steel Trust. That was last summer. He was on his horse when I saw him leisurely riding in the middle of the street, while the strikers lined the sidewalks and gazed on, some bitterly, some curiously. Many had never seen the Cossacks before. On one side of his saddle was a large elub, longer than the ordinary cop's club. On the other side in a "boot" hung a rifle, or "carbine," as they call them in the army. Attached to his belt was a "six shooter," filled to the muzzle. And around his belt and in his saddle bags he had probably three hundred "rounds" of ammunition. I was surprised when I saw him, He noticed me first and spoke. "Tim" I knew well. We had "soldiered" in the same reginent of cavalry together. That was when I was patriotic (?). He "bunked" next me for many months, and we slept out in the open together. I was somewhat surprised to see "Tim" a Cos sack, because I looked upon him as somewhat of a "rebel." It was one of his hobbies while in the "service" to kid a recruit by asking him if he "held his hand up" because he "got patriotic," and then while waiting for the poor "rookie" to think out an answer, to say: "Yes, like the rest of us-you got hungry. That's the kind of a man "Tim" was, He was worse than that. He was sent to Butler, Pa., before he came to New Castle. There he, with about ten others of his kind, rode down the strikers and beat them with heavy clubs. They did the same thing at New Castle. I saw them. About eight of these men on horseback' one day last summer came a galloping out of the enclosure of the tin mills and charged into about 500 strikers that were parading the streets, the strikers having had their pictures taken near the mills a few minutes before. The strikers should have waited until the Cossacks got through with them and then had the fellow with the camera do his stunt, I thought. These brutal troopers rushed into, that crowd of harmless (too harmless) men and boys and dropped heads in every direction. PREAMBLE The working class and the employing class have nothing in common. There can be no peace so long as hunger and want are found among millions of working people and the few, who make up the employing class, have all the good things of life.. Between these two classes a struggle must go on until the workers of the world organize as a class, take possession of the earth and the machinery of production, and abolish the wage system. We find that the centering of the management of industries into fewer and fewer hands makes the trade unions unable to cope with the ever-growing power of the employing class. The trade unions foster a state of affairs which allows one set of workers to be pitted against another set of workers in the same industry, thereby helping defeat one another in wage wars. Moreover, the trade unions aid the employing class to mislead the workers into the belief that the working class have interests in common with their employers. These conditions can be changed and the interest of the working class upheld only by an organization formed in such a way that all its members in any one industry, or in all industries if necessary, cease work whenever a strike or lockout is on in any department thereof, thus making an injury to one an injury to all. Instead of the conservative motto, "A fair day's wages for a fair day's work," we must inscribe on our banner the revolutionary watchword, "Abolition of the wage system," It is the historic mission of the working class to do away with capitalism. The army of production must be organized, not only for the every-day struggle with capitalists, but also to carry on production 'when capitalism shall have been overthrown, By organizing industrially we are forming the structure of the new society within the shell of the old. INDUSTRIAL DEMOCRACY Is it a Dream, Possibility, or Embryonic Reality? Read and Ponder. Industrial Democracy is it a dream or a possibility? The writer has recently witnessed some things that lead him to believe it is more than both an embryonic reality! It was on Friday evening, Aug. 26th, the day the lawyers representing both sides met, drafted and made public a settlement of the big cloakmakers' strike in New York City. The writer was passing through Union Square Park, near the heart of the cloak-making center, un aware of the latter fact. Suddenly he be came conscious that something important was afoot. Eager men swarmed about the Yiddisher newsboys, and gobbled up the evening newspapers as fast as they could be handed out. The columns were feverishly scanned, and the whole atmosphere seemed tense and surcharged. Four blocks downtownward, at E. 10th St. and Fourth avenue, a speaker was addressing from the first-story office of the Cloak and Skirtmakers Union a crowd of Jewish men and women that jammed the street from eurb to curb Excitement was once more evident. What the speaker said could not be understood by the writer; but it was plain to be seen that he was forcible and earnest, with no trivial message to deliver. Next day, Saturday, Aug. 27th, the writer was again passing through Union Square park; this time in the early morning. Groups of men could be seen in animated discussion. In the center, one of the number was reading from a Yiddish Argument followed. Then newspaper. another reference to the paper, followed by more argument, sometimes individual, sometimes with the tumult of the whole group. Going up Broadway and looking westward through the side streets, large groups of men and women could be observed before the tall modern loft buildings, all consulting the press and discussing. On Wes: 26th, the bicycle policemen, who have been on strike duty steadily for seven weeks, were drawn into the turmoil. Forgetting their duty momentarily, they joined the groups of strikers and listened intently, while one of the latter, newspaper in hand, dwelt on the terms of agreement drawn up by the lawyers, and their significance to the strikers. It was an absorbing topic to all concerned. In the afternoon, going down Fourth avenue, at Twelfth street, a long line of strikers, headed by women carrying American flags, was encountered. They turned into East 11th St., where they were received with applause and cheers by a block full of people, mostly, men. A large portion of them were hurrying in and out of Webster Hall, in whose big interior the Cutters' local was voting on the terms of settlement. A still larger number was formed in line, ready to parade. At their head was a hanner, bearing the legend: "We protest against the conference; we want the closed shop." They were bound for the headquarters of the conference committee on West $4th St., there to demonstrate against the accept ance of the terms of settlement drawn up by the lawyers. They marched off shouting: "No open shop! No open shop! No open shop! No open shop!" As they marched away, the writer, still hearing the words, "No open shop; no open shop, faintly in the distance, asked himself: What does it all mean? Whe should whole streets full of men and women excite themselves unduly over the open shop? TEXTILE SITUATION Ares- The situation in the textile industry continues to grow more favorable in the woolen branch, with the cotton end prać- tically unchanged, if not s'ightly worse. Fifteen thousand employes of the Amoskeag Cotton Manufacturing Company were thrown out of work on Aug. 26, when the time schedules since spring, have resumed operations on full time. The woolen mills at Almyville, Plainfield, Central Village and other Connecticut points have resumed in full and at Central Village the Central Worsted Mills are running until 9 P. M. daily. Another mill which will run overtime is the Somerset Mill of Monson, Mass.. which went on a day and night schedule. The Berkshire Woolen & Worsted Company's mills in Pittsfield, have again started work after a long shutdown. After a three weeks' shutdown the Gonie Woolen Mill in Rochester, N. Н., got into motion. The Manchester, Eng. Federation of Master Cotton Spinners, which controls the greater part of the cotton spinning industry, has issued a report for the vear ending June 50. The exceptional high price of raw material, resulting from crop failures, is given as the cause of "a condition of things without parallel during recent years." As a remedy, "curtailment of production" is held to be absolutely necessary. From July 10, 1909 to April 25, 1910, there was an equivalent stoppage of 651 hours, in the mills of "the American section of the Federation members, together with many outside firms." The report continues: "The depression in the cotton trade has been universal, and, through the agency of the International Federation, short-time working has also been adopted by practically all the European Continental countries and the United States of America. At the time of issuing this report short-time working on an organized basis has been discontinued in this country, although individual curtailment is still being carried out on a very large scale. At the recent International Congress, held at Brussels from June 6 to 9, the opinion was unanimously expressed that curtailment of production should be continued, and it is quite clear that whether or not further steps are taken to curtail production on an organized basis many mills will be forced either to shut down for a prolonged period or to systematically reduce their consumption of cot- Since June 30 this has been practically the case.
